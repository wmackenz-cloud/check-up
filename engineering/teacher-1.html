Teacher Notes 1: What Is It and What Does It Do?

Open the student page

Purpose: give mechanical and structural engineers the core language to name a component and explain it in a meeting, an email or on site: what it is, what it is made of, what it consists of, how it is attached or connected, and what it does (supports, transmits, prevents ... from + -ing, is used to, is designed to).

Lesson time: 50 minutes, plus homework.

Level: B1 to B2. Before the lesson: ask the student to bring (or photograph) one machine, drawing or structure from their own work. It gives you real material for the speaking task and the homework.

Lesson plan (50 minutes)

  1. Warm-up (5 min). The three warm-up questions. Note which parts the student already names in English and which they describe with gestures.
  2. Key vocabulary (10 min). Go through the function phrases first, then the parts. Ask the student to point to each part on their own photo or drawing, or on something in the room (a chair has joints, a desk lamp has a spring, a door has hinges and screws). Drill pronunciation (see below).
  3. Exercises 1 and 2 (8 min). Matching, then the function phrases. In Exercise 2, push for the form after each phrase: used to + verb, used for + -ing, prevent ... from + -ing.
  4. Exercise 3 or 4 (5 min). Do one in class and set the other for homework, depending on the student’s speed.
  5. Reading (8 min). The email about the conveyor. Ask the student to underline every function phrase first, then answer the questions. Follow-up: “Draw the conveyor from the description.” A good drawing shows real understanding.
  6. Speaking (10 min). Questions 2 and 3 are the core. The student describes their own machine or structure for 1 to 2 minutes; you ask follow-up questions as a new colleague would (“What’s that part for?” “What’s it made of?” “How is it attached?”).
  7. Prepositions and wrap-up (4 min). Exercise 7 Part A quickly; Part B and the homework at home.

Key vocabulary: notes

Pronunciation

  • component: comPOnent /kəmˈpəʊnənt/. Many students stress the first or last syllable.
  • column: /ˈkɒləm/. The final n is silent.
  • bearing: BEAring /ˈbeərɪŋ/, like bear, not beer.
  • shaft /ʃɑːft/ (North American /ʃæft/): the sh sound, not s.
  • welded, connected: after d or t, the -ed is an extra syllable (/ˈweldɪd/). In attached and fixed it is not: /əˈtætʃt/, /fɪkst/.
  • truss /trʌs/, brace /breɪs/, bracket /ˈbrækɪt/: students often mix brace and bracket. A brace is a structural diagonal; a bracket is a small support part.

False friends and common mistakes

  • detail: in Russian, Ukrainian and Polish (деталь, detal) this means a machine part. In English, a detail is a small fact or a drawing of a connection (a connection detail). For the object, say part or component.
  • piece: Spanish pieza, Italian pezzo, French pièce. In English, piece is a bit of something (a piece of steel). For a machine part, say part.
  • construction: in many languages this means “structure” or “design” (German Konstruktion). In English it usually means the building process. Say the structure or the design.
  • is consisted of: very common. Consist of is never passive and never -ing in this meaning: The truss consists of ... (B2: is made up of, is composed of are passive and fine).
  • is used to + verb (purpose) vs be used to + -ing (accustomed). A washer is used to spread the load vs I’m used to working on site. Mention it only if the student mixes them.
  • prevent to do: say prevent something from doing. Also avoid is not a synonym here: a guard prevents accidents; people avoid danger.
  • footing vs foundation: foundation is the general word for everything below the structure; a footing is one type (a widened base under a column or wall). Structural students will know this; mechanical students may not.

Note that bearing means different things: a machine bearing lets a shaft turn; a bridge bearing sits between the deck and the pier and lets the deck move a little (for example in hot weather). Structural students enjoy this one.

Speaking prompts and follow-ups

  • Describe an object like an engineer. Follow up: “Which part would fail first? Why?” “How would you make it lighter?”
  • Describe your machine or structure to a new colleague. Follow up as the colleague: “Sorry, what’s that part for?” “Is it bolted or welded?” “What’s it connected to?”
  • What happens if a small part is missing? Good for prevent ... from: “Without the washer, the nut could damage the surface.” “Without the braces, the frame could sway in the wind.”
  • Interview question. Coach the structure: the project in one sentence, what the system consisted of, their role, one result. Keep it to about one minute.

Answers

  • Exercise 1: shaft = transmits power from the motor; bearing = lets a shaft turn with low friction; washer = spreads the load under a nut; valve = controls the flow of water or gas; spring = stores energy and absorbs shocks; column = carries loads down to the foundation; footing = spreads the load from a column into the soil; brace = stops a frame from moving sideways.
  • Exercise 2: 1 support, 2 transmits, 3 prevents, 4 consists of, 5 is made of, 6 to spread, 7 absorbing, 8 to spread.
  • Exercise 3: 1 nut, 2 bolt, 3 weld, 4 gear, 5 slab, 6 column, 7 joint, 8 truss.
  • Exercise 4: 1 The shaft is supported by two bearings. 2 A washer is used to spread the load. 3 The beam is connected to the column with bolts. 4 What is this bracket made of? 5 This valve prevents oil from flowing back into the tank.
  • Exercise 5: 1 boxes, 2 a gearbox, 3 two bearings, 4 aluminium, 5 clothing. The writing task is checked by Claude.
  • Exercise 7: Part A: of: consists of, made of, part of; to: attached to, connected to, welded to, designed to; for: used for, suitable for, responsible for. Part B: 1 consists of, 2 connected to, 3 used for absorbing, 4 no mistake, 5 designed to spread.
