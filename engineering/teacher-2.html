Teacher Notes 2: Materials and Properties

Open the student page

Purpose: help the student describe common engineering materials and their properties accurately, build word families (strength / strong / strengthen), and compare materials in a design discussion (lighter than, not as stiff as, twice as strong as).

Lesson time: 50 minutes, plus homework.

Level: B1 to B2. Before the lesson: ask the student which materials they use most and one material decision they have made or seen (why steel and not aluminium, why precast and not cast-in-place, and so on).

Lesson plan (50 minutes)

  1. Warm-up (5 min). The three warm-up questions. The road salt question works well with anyone who lives in Toronto: parking garages and bridges are full of corrosion examples.
  2. Key vocabulary (10 min). Materials, then properties. Spend time on the pairs students mix up: strong vs stiff, ductile vs brittle, flexible vs elastic (see below). Then the word family table: ask the student to say each row aloud.
  3. Exercises 1 and 2 (8 min). Matching, then the word-family sort and the gap fill. In Exercise 2, ask “What comes before the gap? So, noun, adjective or verb?” before they type.
  4. Exercise 3 (5 min). Comparatives. Encourage the optional “Your turn” sentence with a material from their own work.
  5. Reading (8 min). The footbridge email. After the questions, ask: “What other information would you want before you decide?” (cost over the whole life, maintenance, appearance, fabrication time, vibration checks).
  6. Speaking (10 min). Question 2 is the core: compare two materials for one real job. Then question 3 or the challenge question, depending on time.
  7. Wrap-up (4 min). Exercise 4 or Exercise 7 Part A quickly; the rest and the homework at home.

Key vocabulary: notes

Engineering meaning (check the student’s understanding, not only the English)

  • Strong vs stiff. Strength = how much load before it fails. Stiffness = how much it deflects under load. Aluminium alloys can be quite strong, but aluminium is only about a third as stiff as steel (elastic modulus about 70 GPa vs 200 GPa), so an aluminium beam bends more. Even engineers mix these words in English.
  • Ductile vs brittle. Ductile materials (steel, aluminium, copper) stretch and give a warning before they fail. Brittle materials (glass, cast iron, plain concrete) fail suddenly. This is one reason design codes aim for ductile behaviour.
  • Heavy vs light is about density: steel about 7850 kg/m³, concrete about 2400 kg/m³, aluminium about 2700 kg/m³, softwood timber about 450 to 550 kg/m³.
  • Concrete is about ten times as strong in compression as in tension; that is why it is reinforced.

False friends and common mistakes

  • resistance: Spanish and Portuguese resistencia / resistência, Italian resistenza are often used for strength. In English, resistance needs to: resistance to corrosion / heat / fatigue. For load-carrying capacity, say strength: the compressive strength of the concrete.
  • armature: Italian armatura, Russian арматура mean steel reinforcement. In English, an armature is part of an electric motor. Say reinforcement or rebar.
  • beton (many European languages): in English it is concrete. Cement is only the powder in concrete, not the finished material.
  • elastic vs flexible: elastic in engineering means it returns to its shape after the load is removed (elastic behaviour); flexible means it bends easily. A steel beam is elastic but not flexible.
  • hard vs stiff vs strong: hardness is resistance to scratching or denting (surfaces, tools). Do not use hard to mean stiff or strong.
  • timber vs lumber: in Canada, lumber is sawn wood you buy (2×4s); timber is used for structural wood and large members (mass timber, timber frame). Both are correct.
  • more lighter, as stiff than, twice stronger: the three most common comparative mistakes. Say much lighter, as stiff as, twice as strong as. Avoid three times lighter in reports: a third of the weight is clearer.

Pronunciation

  • aluminium /ˌæljʊˈmɪniəm/ (five syllables, stress on MIN) and aluminum /əˈluːmɪnəm/ (four syllables, stress on LU). Both are correct; Canadian engineers usually say and write aluminum. The site uses aluminium; the typed answers accept both.
  • strength /streŋθ/ and length /leŋθ/: one syllable, with ng + th at the end. Many students say strengt or strenk.
  • durable /ˈdjʊərəbəl/ but durability /ˌdjʊərəˈbɪləti/: the stress moves. The same with flexible / flexibility.
  • composite: North American comPOSite, British COMposite. ductile: North American /ˈdʌktəl/, British /ˈdʌktaɪl/.
  • steel /stiːl/ vs still /stɪl/: a long vowel. concrete: stress on the first syllable, CONcrete.

Speaking prompts and follow-ups

  • Strengths and weaknesses of a material. Follow up: “How do you protect it?” “What happens to it in a Canadian winter?”
  • Compare two materials for one job. Push for three comparing structures in one answer. Follow up: “And which is cheaper over 30 years?”
  • Road salt. Expected ideas: salt water reaches the rebar, the rebar corrodes and expands, the concrete cracks and spalls. Protection: more concrete cover, waterproof membranes, epoxy-coated or stainless rebar, sealers, regular washing. (Lesson 5 returns to this as a problem-and-solution topic.)
  • Tall timber buildings. Good for opinions and reasons. Facts to help: large timber members char on the outside and keep their strength inside for a long time, and these buildings must meet the same fire safety requirements as other tall buildings.

Answers

  • Exercise 1: steel = strong and ductile, but rusts if not protected; concrete = strong in compression, weak in tension; reinforced concrete = concrete with steel bars to carry tension; timber = light and renewable, but must be kept dry; aluminium = light and corrosion-resistant, but less stiff than steel; cast iron = strong in compression, but brittle; composites = glass or carbon fibre in a plastic resin.
  • Exercise 2: Sort: nouns strength, length, flexibility, durability, resistance; adjectives strong, flexible, resistant; verbs strengthen, lengthen, resist, stiffen. Gaps: 1 strengthen, 2 strength, 3 resistant, 4 resistance, 5 length, 6 flexibility, 7 durable, 8 stiffen.
  • Exercise 3: 1 lighter than, 2 as stiff as, 3 not as strong as, 4 as strong, 5 more brittle, 6 the lightest, 7 much weaker than, 8 more.
  • Exercise 4: 1 Aluminium is lighter than steel but less stiff. 2 Concrete is strong in compression but weak in tension. 3 Reinforced concrete is much stronger in tension than plain concrete. 4 Stainless steel is more resistant to corrosion than carbon steel. 5 Which material is the most suitable for this bridge?
  • Exercise 5: 1 24, 2 salt, 3 stiff, 4 C, 5 twice. The opinion task is checked by Claude.
  • Exercise 7: Part A: 1 to, 2 in, 3 of, 4 from, 5 for, 6 with, 7 between, 8 to. Part B: 1 lighter, 2 as stiff as, 3 no mistake, 4 strengthen, 5 as stiff as.
