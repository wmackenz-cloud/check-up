Teacher Notes 3: Measurements, Numbers and Data

Open the student page

Purpose: the student gives dimensions and measurements clearly, says numbers and units correctly (decimals, fractions, powers, tolerances, SI units), uses approximation language, and describes trends in test data in a meeting or a short email.

Level: B1 to B2. Lesson time: 50 minutes, plus homework.

Before the lesson: ask the student to bring one real number-heavy item from work: a drawing with dimensions and tolerances, a test result, or a graph (with anything confidential removed). It makes Exercise 6 and the homework much more real.

Why this lesson matters

  • Engineers usually know the maths perfectly. What breaks down in English is saying the numbers: decimal commas, “two comma five”, unclear units, or a long pause before every figure. In a meeting this sounds less competent than the engineer really is.
  • Errors with numbers are expensive. “Fifteen” and “fifty”, or 1,500 read as “one point five”, can cause real mistakes on site or in an order.
  • Trend language (rose sharply, levelled off, peaked at) is what students need to present test results, monitoring data or progress figures.

50-minute lesson plan

  1. Warm-up (5 min). The three warm-up questions. Listen to how the student says the units they name. Note problems for later; don't correct yet.
  2. Numbers drill (10 min). Go through the “Saying numbers and units” table aloud. Then dictate 8 to 10 numbers fast for the student to write (2.5, 0.75, 1,500, 3/4, 10³, ± 0.05 mm, 35 MPa, 150 kN, 13 / 30, 15 / 50). Then swap: the student dictates, you write. Exercise 1 can be done here or as homework.
  3. Dimensions and forces (10 min). Key vocabulary rows 1–11, then Exercises 2 and 3 (sorter). Ask the student to describe something on their desk or a part they know: “It's about 40 mm wide and 5 mm thick.”
  4. Trends (12 min). Exercise 4 (the concrete graph). Then the student describes the whole graph aloud for one minute without looking at the sentences. Push for the prepositions: a rise of / in, increased by, from… to, peaked at.
  5. Reading (8 min). Exercise 5. Ask: “Would you send this email to your manager? What would you add?” Then one or two of the speaking questions (Exercise 6).
  6. Wrap-up (5 min). Exercise 7 Part B together if time (the common errors), set the homework and Exercise 7 for home.

Key vocabulary: notes

Pronunciation

  • diameter /daɪˈæmɪtər/: stress on the second syllable, di-A-me-ter. Students often stress the first or the third.
  • width, depth, length end in /θ/: /wɪdθ/, /depθ/, /leŋθ/. Many students drop the /θ/, so width sounds like wit. height /haɪt/ has no /θ/ (not “heighth”).
  • tolerance: TOL-er-ance. approximately: ap-PROX-i-mate-ly. compression: com-PRESS-ion. kilometre: KIL-o-me-tre or ki-LOM-e-tre (both are heard in Canada).
  • analysis / analyse: a-NAL-y-sis but AN-a-lyse. data: DAY-ta or DA-ta, both fine. In engineering English, the data is and the data are are both used.
  • -teen / -ty: fif-TEEN vs FIF-ty. When it matters, engineers say “one five” or “five zero”, or repeat the number. Teach this as a real strategy.
  • rise (noun) /raɪz/ ends in a /z/ sound; price, rice end in /s/.

False friends and common errors

  • Decimal comma. Most European and Latin American languages write 2,5. In English it is 2.5 (“two point five”), and the comma (or a space, in SI style) separates thousands. This is the most important point in the lesson.
  • billion = 10⁹ (a thousand million) in modern English. In several languages (Italian bilione, German Billion, French billion, Spanish billón) the similar word means 10¹². 10⁹ is miliardo / Milliarde / milliard / mil millones.
  • rise / raise. The temperature raised is a very common error. Rise (rose, risen) has no object; raise needs one.
  • a 6-metre beam (compound adjective, no -s) vs 6 metres long. Also a 40-tonne crane, a 200 mm slab (with symbols, the hyphen is usually dropped).
  • The beam has 6 metres (from ha 6 metri, tiene 6 metros): in English, is 6 metres long or has a length of 6 metres.
  • tonne / ton. A tonne (metric ton) is 1,000 kg. In North America a ton often means a short ton (about 907 kg). In SI documents, use tonne (t).
  • stress in engineering is force per area (MPa). Students may know only the everyday meaning (feeling worried).

Accuracy notes if a student asks: the concrete graph is typical of normal Portland cement concrete (roughly 65–70% of the 28-day strength at 7 days). 2.4 kPa is the usual National Building Code of Canada live load for a parking garage for cars. A beam on two simple supports under downward load has compression at the top and tension at the bottom; in a cantilever it is the other way round, which is a good B2 follow-up question.

Answers

  • Exercise 1, Say it right: 2.5 m = two point five metres; 1/4 = a quarter; 10³ = ten cubed; ± 0.5 mm = plus or minus zero point five millimetres; 35 MPa = thirty-five megapascals; 150 kN = one hundred and fifty kilonewtons; 12 m² = twelve square metres; 1,500 = one thousand five hundred.
  • Exercise 2, Dimensions: 1 deep, 2 width, 3 thick, 4 height, 5 long, 6 radius, 7 span, 8 tolerance.
  • Exercise 3, Tension or compression? Tension: the main cable of a suspension bridge, the bottom of a beam on two supports, a bolt after it is tightened, the chain of a crane when it lifts a load, a tie in a roof truss. Compression: a column that carries a floor, the top of a beam on two supports, the legs of a workbench, a strut in a roof truss, a concrete pier under a bridge.
  • Exercise 4, Describe the graph: 1 sharply, 2 just under, 3 of, 4 40%, 5 just over, 6 by, 7 began to level off. The “Your turn” sentences are checked by Claude and arrive with the student's answers.
  • Exercise 5, Reading: 1 six, 2 78 (dB), 3 48 (°C), 4 levelled (off), 5 start-up, 6 tolerance. The writing task (describe your own data) is checked by Claude.
  • Exercise 7, Part A: 1 in, 2 by, 3 to, 4 at, 5 of, 6 in, 7 for.
  • Exercise 7, Part B: 1 We ordered three 12-metre beams for the new roof. 2 No mistake. 3 The result was two point five millimetres. 4 The temperature rose by 5 degrees in an hour. 5 The beam is 6 metres long. 6 The temperature rose sharply after the fan stopped. 7 No mistake. 8 The tolerance is plus or minus 0.1 mm.
  • Exercise 7, Part C: 1 The temperature rose sharply in the first hour. 2 The shaft has a diameter of 50 mm. 3 There was a rise of 6 MPa in strength. 4 The bridge has a main span of 45 metres. 5 Pressure fell by 15% after the valve was adjusted. Time phrases and the after clause may also go at the start.

Speaking prompts

  • Read these aloud: 0.025 mm, 1/8, 2.5 × 10⁶, 4,750 kg, −15 °C, 12.5 kN/m, 3/4 inch.
  • Describe the size of your desk, your car or a part you know well, without using your hands.
  • Tell me about a time a measurement was wrong. What happened, and how did you find out?
  • Look at the gearbox email again. Present the results to me as if I were the manager in a meeting. I will ask two questions.
  • B2: What is the difference between accuracy and precision? Give an example from your work.
  • B2: Which is better for presenting test results to a client: a table or a graph? Why?
