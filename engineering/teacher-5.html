Teacher Notes 5: Problems and Solutions

Open the student page

Purpose: give engineers the language to describe a fault, explain its cause and effect, say how sure they are about the cause, and recommend a solution in a meeting, an email or a job interview.

Lesson time: 50 minutes, plus homework (a problem-report email).

Before the lesson: ask the student to bring one real problem from their work: a machine that failed, a crack, a leak, a delay on site. A photo or a short description is enough. You will use it in the role play (Exercise 5) and the homework.

Lesson plan (50 minutes)

  1. Warm-up (5 min). The three warm-up questions. Listen for how the student describes a problem now; note one or two gaps to return to.
  2. Key vocabulary and Exercise 1 (8 min). Go through the table quickly; the student will know most of the ideas in their own language. Focus on pronunciation and word forms (below). Exercise 1 can be done before the lesson.
  3. Cause and effect, Exercise 2 (8 min). Draw an arrow on the board: CAUSE → RESULT. led to / resulted in / caused go forwards along the arrow; due to / because of / caused by go backwards. Do the sorter together, then Part B.
  4. Diagnosis modals, Exercise 3 (7 min). Show a photo of a fault (or the student's own problem) and ask “What's the cause?” Elicit must be / might be / can't be with a reason each time. Stress that mustn't is not used for this.
  5. Reading, Exercise 4 (8 min). The student reads the email and answers. Then ask: “Find the cause, the effects and the recommendation.” The email models the four-step structure for the role play and homework.
  6. Recommending, Exercise 6 (6 min). Run through the Watch out box and Part A. Parts B and C can be homework.
  7. Role play, Exercise 5 (8 min). You are the manager in a weekly meeting. The student reports their own problem in four steps: what, cause, effect, solution. Push back once (“Can't we wait until next month?”) so they must defend the recommendation with It would be better to… because…

Exercise 7 (prepositions and word forms) is good homework or a warm-up for the next lesson.

Key vocabulary: pronunciation and traps

WordNotes
corrosion, corrodeStress on the second syllable: cor-RO-sion /kəˈroʊʒən/, cor-RODE. The s is /ʒ/ as in measure. Adjective: corroded (not “corrosioned”).
fatiguefa-TIGUE /fəˈtiːɡ/, rhymes with league. False friend risk: for people it means tiredness; in engineering it is cracking under repeated (cyclic) loading, often well below the material's strength.
misalignment, misalignedThe g is silent: mis-a-LIGN-ment /ˌmɪsəˈlaɪnmənt/.
vibration, vibratevi-BRA-tion /vaɪˈbreɪʃən/; the verb is VI-brate in North America (vi-BRATE in British English).
deflectionde-FLEC-tion. Not the same as deformation (a change of shape) or displacement (movement); in a beam, deflection is the vertical bending under load.
settlementSET-tle-ment. Students may know it only as “a small village” or “an agreement”.
wear, wornIrregular: wear, wore, worn. Pronounced like where, not like were.
failure, failStudents often say “the bearing is failure”. Practise: the bearing failed / a bearing failure.
recommendrec-om-MEND, stress on the last syllable. Only one c, double m.
analysis, analyse; diagnosis, diagnosea-NAL-y-sis but AN-a-lyse; di-ag-NO-sis but DI-ag-nose. Plurals: analyses, diagnoses (/-siːz/). Canadian spelling: analyze is common; the site uses analyse.

False friends to watch

  • control: in many languages (Spanish controlar, German Kontrolle, Polish kontrola, Russian контроль) it means check or inspect. In English, control usually means operate or regulate. “We controlled the welds” should be We inspected / checked the welds.
  • armature: Russian арматура, French armature, Italian armatura = the steel bars in concrete. In English: reinforcement, rebar, reinforcing steel. (An English armature is part of an electric motor.)
  • eventually: in many languages eventual(ly) means “possibly”. In English it means “in the end”. “The beam can eventually fail” should be The beam could fail.
  • actual / actually: means “real / really”, not “current / now”.
  • defect vs default: a defect is a fault; a default is a standard setting (or not paying a debt).

Common grammar errors

  • caused from → caused by; resulted to → resulted in; the reason of → the reason for; the cause for → the cause of.
  • because the corrosion → because of the corrosion (because + clause; because of + noun).
  • It mustn't be the seal → It can't be the seal (deduction, not prohibition).
  • I recommend to replace, I suggest you to replace → I recommend replacing / I suggest (that) we replace.

Adapting the lesson

ElementB1B2
Cause and effectbecause, because of, caused by, led toAdd resulted from vs resulted in, as a consequence, attributed to, contributed to (a partial cause)
DiagnosisPresent: must be, might be, can't beAdd past deduction for failure reports: The bolt must have been overtightened. It can't have been fatigue.
RecommendingI recommend + -ing, we should, it would be better toAdd hedging for reports and meetings: It may be worth…, I'd suggest…, One option would be to…, and disagreeing politely
Role playUse the four-step prompts in the boxNo notes; you challenge cost, safety and downtime

Mechanical vs structural students

  • Mechanical: stay with pumps, gearboxes, conveyors, compressors. Useful extras: runout, backlash, play, seize (seized), clog (clogged), contamination, lubrication, coupling, vibration analysis, thermography, preventive vs predictive maintenance.
  • Structural: swap the pump email for a parking garage or bridge. Useful extras: spalling, delamination, efflorescence, rust staining, chlorides, freeze-thaw damage, honeycombing, excessive deflection, differential settlement, condition assessment, rehabilitation. Toronto parking garages are a classic case: road salt brings chlorides into the slab, the reinforcing steel corrodes and expands, and the concrete cracks and spalls.
  • Both: a good interview question is “Tell me about a failure you investigated.” Coach the structure problem → cause → action → result.

Answers

  • Exercise 1: crack = a thin break line; leak = liquid escaping; wear = material lost by rubbing; fatigue = damage from a repeated load; deflection = how much a beam bends; misalignment = shafts not in line; settlement = a foundation moving down; overheating = hotter than is safe.
  • Exercise 2: Part A cause after: due to, caused by, because of, as a result of. Result after: resulted in, led to, caused, as a result. Part B: 1 due to, 2 led to, 3 caused by, 4 because, 5 resulted in, 6 because of. Each Part B item has a “Your turn” box (Claude checks the student's own sentence).
  • Exercise 3: 1 must, 2 can't, 3 might, 4 must, 5 can't, 6 might, 7 must.
  • Exercise 4: 1 vibration, 2 three, 3 inspected (checked), 4 motor, 5 realigning. Opinion question checked by Claude. (Technical note: 9.2 mm/s RMS vibration velocity is in the highest zone of the ISO 10816 / 20816 severity charts for most process pumps, where damage is likely, so a planned shutdown is the right call; misalignment after a motor change is a very common cause of bearing overheating and seal leaks.)
  • Exercise 6: Part A: 1 replacing, 2 check, 3 better to wait, 4 correct, 5 that you inspect, 6 replace, 7 correct, 8 tighten. Part B: see the student answer key. Part C: open (Claude checks).
  • Exercise 7: Part A: in, to, with, of, to, from, in, with. Part B: corroded, worn, deflected, failed, overheats, settled, misaligned, vibrates.

Speaking prompts

  • What is the most common problem with the machines or structures you work on? What usually causes it?
  • Tell me about a time you found the root cause of a problem. How did you find it?
  • “It's cheaper to repair things when they break.” Do you agree? When is preventive maintenance worth it?
  • Your manager wants to delay a repair to save money. What do you say?
  • Describe a famous engineering failure you know about. What was the cause?
  • How do you report a safety problem at your workplace? Who do you tell first?
