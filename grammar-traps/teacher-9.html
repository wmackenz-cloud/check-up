Teacher Notes 9: Relative Clauses

Open the student worksheet

Why students get this wrong

Many languages use one relative word for people and things (French qui/que, Spanish que, Swedish som), so learners say the man which. Arabic, Persian and some other languages repeat the pronoun inside the clause, giving the hotel where we stayed in it. And the comma rule for non-defining clauses doesn't exist in most languages, so learners use that after commas.

How to explain it

Say: "A relative clause is a way to join two sentences. It adds information about a noun. First, look at the noun to choose the word: a person is who, a thing is which, a place is where, an owner is whose."

On necessary vs extra, say: "Ask: if I remove this information, do we still know which one? The man who lives next door is a doctor. Remove it, and we don't know which man. That's necessary: no commas. My father, who is 80, still plays tennis. Remove it, and we still know who: I only have one father. That's extra: commas, like brackets."

Test questions: Is the noun a person, thing, place or owner? If I remove the clause, do we still know which one?

Rule to repeat: "After a comma, never that."

If the student asks...

  • "When can I use that?" Only in necessary clauses, for people or things. It's very common in speech. In extra (comma) clauses, never.
  • "Why can I leave out that in The book I'm reading?" Because another subject (I) follows. The relative word is the object, so it can go. When the relative word is the subject (The book that won the prize), it must stay, or the sentence breaks.
  • "Who or whom?" Whom is very formal and used after prepositions (to whom). In speaking and most writing, who is fine. Aptis and Cambridge won't penalise who.
  • "Why My brother, who lives in Spain, with commas?" If you have only one brother, the clause is extra information. Without commas, it would suggest you have several brothers and mean the one in Spain. This is a lovely example to show how commas change meaning.

Exercise notes and answers with reasons

Exercise 1: who, which, where, whose or when

Items 1 to 3 are dictionary-style definitions. Point out that this is how English defines things, which makes it useful for explaining a word you've forgotten in the speaking test.

  1. who: a person.
  2. where: a place.
  3. which: a thing.
  4. whose: his car (possession).
  5. when: a time.
  6. where: a place.
  7. who (or that): a person.
  8. which (or that): a thing.
  9. whose: her father.
  10. when: a time.
  11. who (or that): people.
  12. which (or that): a thing.

Exercise 2: Necessary or extra

For each one, ask: "If I remove the clause, do we know which one?"

  1. My mother, who is a teacher, loves reading. You have only one mother: extra.
  2. No commas. Which teacher? The one who helped me: necessary.
  3. Paris, which is the capital of France, is very beautiful. There's only one Paris: extra.
  4. No commas. Which car? The one I bought: necessary.
  5. My brother, who lives in Spain, is visiting next week. The bracket says one brother: extra.
  6. No commas. Which people? Those who exercise: necessary.

Exercise 3: Join the sentences

Watch for the student repeating the pronoun (which it is). Say: "The relative word replaces it. Don't say it twice."

  1. I have a neighbour who plays the drums every night.
  2. This is the house where I grew up. (Also: which I grew up in.)
  3. She has a dog which / that is afraid of cats.
  4. I met a man whose wife is a famous singer.
  5. London, which is the capital of the UK, is very expensive.
  6. We visited a museum which / that had a great collection of paintings.
  7. My grandmother, who is 95, still cooks every day.
  8. That's the restaurant where we had our wedding dinner.

Exercise 4: Leaving out

Test: is there a new subject straight after the relative word? If yes, you can leave it out.

  1. Can leave out: we follows.
  2. Must keep: called follows; who is the subject.
  3. Can leave out: you follows.
  4. Must keep: leaves follows.
  5. Can leave out: we follows.
  6. Must keep: teaches follows.

Exercise 5: Find and correct the mistake

  1. The book which I'm reading is very good: remove it.
  2. My boss, who is very kind,: no that after a comma.
  3. Correct
  4. a friend who lives: person, so who.
  5. Correct
  6. the man whose house: possession.

Exercises 6 and 7: Definitions and speaking

The guessing game is very effective and students enjoy it. Take turns: you describe an object, the student guesses, then switch. This trains the "explain a word you've forgotten" skill that helps in every speaking test.

Possible answers for Exercise 6: a dentist is a person who looks after your teeth; a kitchen is a room where you cook; an umbrella is a thing which keeps you dry in the rain; a widow is a woman whose husband has died; a birthday is the day when you were born (or when you celebrate being born); an airport is a place where planes take off and land.
