Teacher Notes 4: Countable, Uncountable and Quantifiers

Open the student worksheet

Why students get this wrong

Countability is decided by each language, not by logic. Advice, information, furniture, news and homework are countable in French, Spanish, German, Swedish and many others, so learners say an advice and informations. The few / a few difference is subtle because the words look almost the same but have opposite feelings.

How to explain it

Say: "English sorts nouns into two boxes. Things you count one by one: chairs, ideas, suitcases. And things you measure as a mass: water, money, furniture. The tricky part is that English decides which box, and sometimes it's surprising. You can count chairs, but furniture is a mass. So we learn the tricky ones as a list."

Test question: Can I put a number in front of it? Three advices? No. So it's uncountable.

On few and a few, say: "A few is a glass half full: some, enough, good news. Few on its own is a glass half empty: almost none, not enough, bad news." Draw two glasses.

Contrast pair: "I have a few friends here" (I'm fine). "I have few friends here" (I'm lonely). Ask: "Which person is happier?"

If the student asks...

  • "Why is news singular? It ends in s." It looks plural but it's an uncountable noun, like a mass of information. The news is good. Same with mathematics and physics.
  • "Can I say a coffee?" Yes. Some uncountables become countable when they mean a portion or type: a coffee (a cup), two beers, French wines (types). This is normal in cafés and restaurants.
  • "Is less people really wrong? Native speakers say it." Many native speakers do say it, and it's common in speech. But in exams and formal writing, fewer is the expected form with countable nouns. Teach fewer for the exam.
  • "Why How much does it cost, not How many?" We're asking about money, and money is uncountable. Dollars are countable: How many dollars?

Exercise notes and answers with reasons

Exercise 1: C or U

Pairs to point out: suitcase / luggage, table / furniture, job / work. Same idea, different box.

  1. C suitcase
  2. U luggage
  3. U advice
  4. C idea
  5. U furniture
  6. C table
  7. U information
  8. C job
  9. U work (in the sense of labour; a work of art is countable)
  10. U traffic
  11. C car
  12. U weather (never a nice weather)

Exercise 2: much or many

Tip: In positive sentences, natural English uses a lot of more than much. I have a lot of time sounds better than I have much time.

  1. much: sugar is uncountable.
  2. many: brothers are countable.
  3. much: milk.
  4. many: friends.
  5. much: price (money).
  6. much: information.
  7. many: people (plural of person).
  8. much: time.

Exercise 3: a few or a little

  1. a few questions: countable.
  2. a little coffee: uncountable.
  3. a little Spanish: a language is uncountable.
  4. a few days: countable.
  5. a little salt: uncountable.
  6. a little help: uncountable.

Exercise 4: few, a few, little, a little

The clues in brackets tell the student the feeling. Remove them for B2 students and make them decide from context.

  1. Few: almost none; the town is quiet.
  2. a few: enough; don't worry.
  3. little: almost none; he can't pay.
  4. a little: enough for a taxi.
  5. few: almost none (very few is a common pattern).
  6. a little: some hope, positive.

Exercise 5: fewer or less

  1. less sugar: uncountable.
  2. fewer cars: countable.
  3. fewer hours: countable.
  4. less money: uncountable.
  5. fewer students: countable.
  6. less free time: uncountable.

Exercise 6: Find and correct the mistake

  1. some good advice or a good piece of advice.
  2. some new furniture: no plural.
  3. Correct: news takes a singular verb.
  4. How much luggage or How many bags.
  5. Correct: work is uncountable here.
  6. some information: no plural.
  7. There are fewer people: two errors here, the verb and the quantifier.
  8. Correct: a piece of makes paper countable.

Exercises 7 and 8: About you and speaking

The town comparison task is excellent Aptis and Cambridge practice: it naturally needs more, fewer and less. Push for at least two of each.
