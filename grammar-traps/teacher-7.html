Teacher Notes 7: Must, Might, Can't (Modals of Deduction)

Open the student worksheet

Why students get this wrong

Students usually learn must first as obligation (You must wear a seatbelt), so they assume the negative is mustn't. In deduction, the negative is can't. The past form (modal + have + past participle) is also unusual; learners write must left or can't have went.

How to explain it

Say: "These words show how sure you are, like a detective. Must means 'I'm almost certain it's true, because I have evidence.' Might means 'maybe'. Can't means 'I'm almost certain it's impossible.'"

Draw it: a vertical line from 100% (must) through 50% (might, may, could) to 0% (can't).

Test question: How sure am I, and what's my evidence?

On the mustn't trap, say: "Mustn't is a rule: you're not allowed. You mustn't smoke here. Can't is a conclusion: it's impossible. That can't be Tom, he's in Paris. Different jobs."

On the past form, say: "To look back at the past, add have + past participle. The modal stays the same. She must be tired becomes She must have been tired."

If the student asks...

  • "What's the difference between might, may and could?" For possibility, almost none. May is a little more formal. For exams, treat them as equal.
  • "Can I say couldn't instead of can't?" Yes, couldn't works for impossibility too, especially in the past: He couldn't have done it. Can't is more common in the present.
  • "What about mustn't have?" It isn't used for deduction. Use can't have.
  • "How does this help in the exam?" It's essential for the photo tasks. Examiners reward speculation: They must be on holiday. She might be waiting for someone.

Exercise notes and answers with reasons

Exercise 1: How sure

  1. YES: must.
  2. MAYBE: might.
  3. NO: can't.
  4. MAYBE: could.
  5. YES: must (the evidence is the long walk).
  6. NO: can't.

Exercise 2: must, might or can't

For each item, ask: "What's the evidence?" The evidence is always in the sentence.

  1. must: evidence, twelve hours of work.
  2. might: no evidence, just a possibility.
  3. can't: impossible at 15.
  4. might: "I don't know".
  5. can't: impossible after a big lunch.
  6. must: evidence, always full.
  7. might: a possibility.
  8. must: evidence, he always comes at ten.
  9. can't: wrong colour, so impossible.
  10. must: evidence, perfect French. Note this one is past (have lived), as a preview of Exercise 3.

Exercise 3: Past deduction

  1. must have rained: evidence, wet ground.
  2. might have been: "I'm not sure".
  3. can't have stolen: impossible, he was with me.
  4. might have left: "Maybe".
  5. can't have seen: evidence, they didn't say hello.
  6. must have cleaned: evidence, very clean.
  7. must have studied: evidence, passed easily.
  8. can't have finished: impossible, 800 pages.

Exercise 4: Rewrite

Map the signal words: I'm sure → must. Perhaps / maybe → might. I'm sure ... not / impossible → can't.

  1. He must be at work.
  2. She might be ill.
  3. That can't be Anna.
  4. They must have forgotten.
  5. I might have left my phone on the bus.
  6. He can't have written this.

Exercise 5: Find and correct the mistake

  1. She can't be at home: deduction, not a rule.
  2. Correct
  3. might have gone: modal + have + past participle.
  4. Correct
  5. Correct: must for a natural conclusion about feelings.
  6. can't have seen: past participle, not past simple.

Exercises 6 and 7: Picture speaking and stories

This is direct exam practice for Aptis Speaking and Cambridge Part 2. Use two or three photos with people doing something unclear. Push the student to give evidence: "She must be cold, because she's wearing a big coat."
