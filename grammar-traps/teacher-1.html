Teacher Notes 1: Present Perfect or Past Simple?

Open the student worksheet

Why students get this wrong

Many languages (French, Spanish, German, Swedish, Italian) use a perfect-looking form for finished past events, so learners say I have seen him yesterday. Other languages have no perfect at all, so learners use the present for unfinished time: I live here since 2019.

How to explain it

Say: "The past simple is a closed box. The time is finished, and we can name it: yesterday, in 2019. The present perfect is an open box. The time reaches up to now, so we don't say exactly when."

Test question: Is the time finished, or does it continue to now?

Draw it: a timeline with NOW on the right. Past simple is a dot in the past, separate from now. Present perfect is a line or arrow from the past that touches NOW.

Contrast pair to say aloud: "I lived in Rome for five years" (I left). "I have lived in Rome for five years" (I still live there). Ask: "Where does this person live now?"

If the student asks...

  • "Why is Shakespeare wrote, not has written?" Shakespeare is dead, so his writing time is finished. Compare: Stephen King has written over 60 novels, because he's alive and still writing.
  • "Why can't I say When have you arrived?" When asks for a specific time, and a specific time is always finished. So when questions almost always take the past simple.
  • "Is this week finished or not?" If you are still in this week, it's unfinished: I have seen him twice this week. On Saturday, talking about last week: I saw him twice last week.
  • "What about American English?" Americans often use the past simple with just, already, yet (Did you eat yet?). For British exams like Aptis and Cambridge, teach the present perfect but don't mark the American form as a big error in speech.

Exercise notes and answers with reasons

Exercise 1: For or since

Tip: If you can ask "How long?" and the answer is a length of time, it's for. If the answer is a moment you can point to on a calendar or clock, it's since.

  1. for: ten years is a length of time.
  2. since: 2021 is a starting point.
  3. since: "he finished university" is a moment in time.
  4. for: a long time is a length.
  5. since: breakfast is a point in the day.
  6. for: six months is a length.
  7. since: last Christmas is a point.
  8. for: forty minutes is a length.

Exercise 2: Choose the correct form

  1. have lost: the result is now (I can't open the door).
  2. wrote: he is dead, so the time is finished.
  3. Did you see: last night is finished.
  4. has never been: her whole life up to now.
  5. bought: in 2018 is a finished time.
  6. haven't finished: yet means up to now.
  7. has broken: "Look!" means we see the result now.
  8. did you meet: when asks for a finished time.
  9. have read: so far this month is unfinished.
  10. left: two years ago is finished.
  11. Have you ever ridden: ever means at any time in your life up to now.
  12. has just started: just means very recently, connected to now.

Exercise 3: Complete with the verb

Watch for: items 6 and 7 are a deliberate pair. Ask the student: "In 6, do I still work here? In 7?"

  1. went: last Tuesday.
  2. has already eaten: the result now is she isn't hungry.
  3. Have you ever tried: life experience.
  4. haven't seen: since the summer, up to now.
  5. moved: when he was five.
  6. have worked (or have been working): I still work here.
  7. worked: then I changed jobs, so it's finished.
  8. Have you finished: yet.
  9. did you get up: what time asks for a finished moment.
  10. have been: they are still married.

Exercise 4: Find and correct the mistake

  1. I met him: last year is finished.
  2. for five years: five years is a length, not a starting point.
  3. Correct: I can't find them now, so the present perfect fits.
  4. We went: yesterday evening is finished.
  5. has worked: since January up to now needs the present perfect, not the present simple.
  6. I have never seen: never before means in my life up to now.
  7. Correct: news connected to now.
  8. When did you start: when asks for a finished time.

Exercise 5: Rewrite the sentence

This is the Cambridge key word transformation format. Remind the student that contractions count as two words in the exam.

  1. have lived here since
  2. have never been to (been to = visited and came back; gone to = is still there)
  3. haven't seen her for
  4. has been working there for or has had his job for
  5. long have you played or long have you been playing
  6. hasn't rained for

Exercises 6 and 7: About you and speaking

Listen for the switch: the student should start with the present perfect (the experience) and move to the past simple as soon as they give details (when, where, what happened). This switch is exactly what examiners notice. If the student stays in the present perfect for the details, ask "When was that?" to push them into the past simple.
