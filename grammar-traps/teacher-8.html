Teacher Notes 8: The Passive

Open the student worksheet

Why students get this wrong

Three common problems: forgetting be altogether (The house built in 1990), using the past simple instead of the past participle (was send, was broke), and confusing passive with continuous (English is speaking here). Some languages make passives with a reflexive (se habla español, on parle français) or a different ending (Swedish -s: talas), so the English two-part form feels heavy.

How to explain it

Say: "In an active sentence, the subject does the action. Someone stole my bike. In a passive sentence, the action happens to the subject. My bike was stolen. We use the passive when the thing is more important than the person, or we don't know who did it."

On form, say: "The passive is always two parts: be + past participle. The past participle never changes. Only be changes, and be carries the tense. So if you can conjugate be, you can make any passive."

Test questions: Did the subject do the action, or did it happen to the subject? What tense do I need, so what form of be?

Demonstrate with one verb across tenses: is cleaned, is being cleaned, was cleaned, has been cleaned, will be cleaned, must be cleaned. Point out that cleaned never changes.

If the student asks...

  • "When should I use by?" Only when the person is important or interesting: The Mona Lisa was painted by Leonardo. Not when it's obvious or unknown: My bike was stolen (by a thief, obviously).
  • "Is the passive formal?" It's more common in formal and written English: news, science, instructions, reports. It's a good way to make Aptis and Cambridge formal writing sound more advanced.
  • "What's the difference between I cut my hair and I had my hair cut?" The first means you used the scissors. The second means someone else did it for you. Many learners say the first when they mean the second.
  • "Can every verb be passive?" Only verbs with an object. Arrive, happen, die can't be passive: The accident was happened.

Exercise notes and answers with reasons

Exercise 1: Past participles

If the student struggles here, stop and drill irregular past participles before going further. The passive is impossible without them.

made, written, sent, built, spoken, stolen, found, broken, chosen, paid.

Exercise 2: Present and past simple passive

  1. is made: general fact, present.
  2. was built: in 1932, past.
  3. are sent: plural subject, present.
  4. was stolen: last night.
  5. was painted: a finished past event.
  6. is grown: a general fact.
  7. were chosen: plural, last week.
  8. is served: a routine.

Exercise 3: Other tenses

Say: "Find the tense first, then change only be."

  1. is being painted: continuous, so be becomes is being.
  2. will be delivered
  3. has been cancelled (US: canceled)
  4. must be worn: modal + be + past participle.
  5. is being built: right now.
  6. have already been sold: already goes between have and been.
  7. should be sent
  8. will be paid

Exercise 4: Active to passive

Steps to teach: 1. Find the object. 2. Make it the subject. 3. Keep the tense with be. 4. Add the past participle. 5. Decide if you need by.

  1. The office is cleaned every evening.
  2. The museum was opened in 1995.
  3. My umbrella has been taken.
  4. The results will be announced next week.
  5. The Harry Potter books were written by J.K. Rowling. (Here by is needed; the author is the important information.)
  6. The road is being repaired.
  7. This medicine must be kept in the fridge.
  8. I wasn't told about the change. (Nobody told me = I was not told.)

Exercise 5: Have something done

  1. I had my car repaired.
  2. She has her hair cut every month.
  3. We are having our windows cleaned.
  4. We will have our photo taken.

Exercise 6: Find and correct the mistake

  1. was broken: past participle, not past simple.
  2. Correct
  3. My wallet was stolen: missing be.
  4. Correct
  5. Spanish is spoken: passive, not continuous.
  6. I'm having my hair cut: someone else will do it.

Exercises 7 and 8: About you and speaking

"A famous product from your country" works well: Volvos are made in Sweden. Maple syrup is produced in Canada. Ask follow-up questions that force the passive: "How is it made? Where is it sold?"
