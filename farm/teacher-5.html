Teacher Notes 5: Looking After Animals

Open the student page

Purpose: give general learners the words for looking after farm animals (feed, milk, graze, groom, shear, muck out), the things they use (trough, hay, straw, feed, milking parlour), farm phrasal verbs (let out, bring in, round up, check on) and routine language: adverbs of frequency and where they go.

Lesson time: 50 minutes, plus a few exercises to finish at home.

Level: B1 to B2. Before the lesson: find a photo of a milking parlour, a sheep being shorn and a sheepdog at work. Optional: a short sheepdog trial video (search “sheepdog trial”). Students love it, and it shows round up and bring in perfectly.

Lesson plan (50 minutes)

  1. Warm-up (5 min). The three warm-up questions. If the student has a pet, use it for routine language straight away: “How often do you feed it?”
  2. Key vocabulary (10 min). Daily jobs first, with photos or mime (milking, brushing, shearing). Then the phrasal verbs: show the pronoun rule on the board: let the hens out / let them out / let out them. Then the frequency box.
  3. Exercises 1 and 2 (8 min). Matching, then routines and phrasal verbs. In Exercise 2, ask the student to explain each adverb position: before the verb, after be.
  4. Exercise 3 or 4 (5 min). Do one together and set the other for home.
  5. Reading (8 min). The volunteer's diary. First ask: “Would you like Jenna's job?” Then the questions. Then ask the student to retell the day in the present simple as Jenna's routine: She always milks the cows first ...
  6. Speaking (10 min). Questions 1 and 2 are the core. Push for a frequency word in every sentence, and at least two phrasal verbs.
  7. Phrasal verbs and wrap-up (4 min). Exercise 7 Part A together; Part B at home.

Key vocabulary: notes

Pronunciation

  • trough /trɒf/ (North American /trɔːf/): rhymes with off. Another -ough word, like plough in Lesson 4, but a different sound.
  • shear /ʃɪə/ sounds like sheer; shorn /ʃɔːn/ rhymes with born. Contrast with share /ʃeə/.
  • calve /kɑːv/: silent l, like calf. lambing /ˈlæmɪŋ/: silent b.
  • muck /mʌk/ rhymes with duck. Watch the vowel: not mock.
  • groom /ɡruːm/ (like room); graze /ɡreɪz/ (like days, with a z sound).
  • Phrasal verbs are stressed on the particle: let them OUT, bring them IN, round them UP.

False friends and common mistakes

  • Adverb position: many languages put the adverb after the verb (She feeds always). Rule: before the main verb, after be. Sometimes can also go at the start.
  • Pronouns with phrasal verbs: let them out, not let out them. But check on does not split: check on them.
  • hay vs straw (from Lesson 4): hay is food, straw is bedding. feed is also a noun for animal food and is uncountable: a bag of feed, not a feed.
  • two times in a day: say twice a day, three times a week, once a month. No in.
  • yesterday night: say last night. Very common from Spanish, French and Chinese speakers.
  • Birth verbs: a cow calves, a ewe lambs, a mare foals. In general English: gives birth to, or a calf was born. Watch for was borned or is born yesterday.
  • vet: in North America also veterinarian; in the UK veterinary surgeon. Vet also means a military veteran in North America.

Adapting the lesson

ElementB1B2
VocabularySix jobs (feed, milk, graze, groom, shear, muck out), the trough and hay/straw, and three phrasal verbs (let out, bring in, round up). Adverbs: always, usually, sometimes, never.All words; add fodder, bedding, livestock auction, to tag, to vaccinate, to wean, and phrasal verbs turn out, see to, put down (also: end an animal's life).
SpeakingDescribe a farmer's day, or your own routine with a pet, using frequency words.Discuss animal welfare: indoor vs outdoor farming, free-range labels, what makes a good life for a farm animal.

Speaking prompts and follow-ups

  • A day on a dairy farm. Follow up: “What does she do first?” “How often are the cows milked?” “Does she ever have a day off?” Listen for adverb position.
  • Looking after an animal. Follow up: “Who took it for walks?” “What did you do when it was ill?” Good for past routines too.
  • A week as a volunteer. Follow up: “What time would you get up?” “Would you muck out the stables?” Encourage reasons with because and so.
  • How animals should be looked after. A B2 stretch: opinions with I think they should ... Follow up: “Would you pay more for milk from cows that graze outside?” This leads into Lesson 6 (free-range, organic).

Answers

  • Exercise 1: muck out = clean the dirty straw out of a stable; groom = brush and clean a horse's coat; shear = cut the wool off a sheep; graze = eat grass in a field; round up = bring animals together into one place; check on = go and see if an animal is OK; trough = a long open container for food or water; vet = a doctor for animals.
  • Exercise 2: 1 always feeds, 2 are always, 3 out, 4 in, 5 round up, 6 straw, 7 muck out, 8 lambing.
  • Exercise 3: 1 graze, 2 trough, 3 vet, 4 parlour, 5 shear, 6 calved, 7 sheepdog, 8 groom.
  • Exercise 4: 1 We usually let the cows out after milking. 2 The vet comes to the farm once a month. 3 How often do you feed the pigs? 4 Bring them in before it gets dark. 5 The sheep are always hungry in winter.
  • Exercise 5: 1 five, 2 milk, 3 litres, 4 limping, 5 sheepdog.
  • Exercise 7: Part A: out: let the hens ___, muck ___ the shed, turn the horses ___ into the field. in: bring the sheep ___, shut the chickens ___ for the night, come ___ out of the rain. up: round ___ the cattle, fill ___ the trough, pick ___ the eggs, clean ___ the yard. Part B: 1 Please let them out before nine. 2 She always feeds the pigs at seven. 3 No mistake. 4 The cows are milked twice a day. 5 We put clean straw on the floor for the horses to sleep on.
