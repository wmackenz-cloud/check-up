Teacher Notes 8: Farm Idioms and Country Life

Open the student page

Purpose: finish the Farm English series with ten everyday farm idioms (make hay while the sun shines, hold your horses, a wild goose chase) and the language for comparing country and city life: -er / more than, not as ... as, much / a lot / a bit and superlatives. It pulls together the animals, places and farm work from the earlier lessons.

Lesson time: 50 minutes, plus a few exercises to finish at home.

Level: B1 to B2. Before the lesson: think of a real story of your own that fits one idiom (a wild goose chase, putting the cart before the horse). Students remember idioms much better from a teacher's story. Ask the student to think about the place they grew up.

Lesson plan (50 minutes)

  1. Warm-up (5 min). The three warm-up questions. Note the comparatives the student uses naturally, and any errors (more cheaper, more good).
  2. Key vocabulary (12 min). Idioms first: tell the farm picture behind each one (why hay needs sun, why cows come home slowly) before the meaning. Then the country and city words, then a quick review of the comparison table.
  3. Exercises 1 and 2 (8 min). Matching idioms, then the comparatives. In Exercise 2, ask the student to say the rule each time: short, long or irregular.
  4. Exercise 3 or 4 (5 min). Do one together and set the other for home.
  5. Reading (7 min). The blog post. First ask: “Find four idioms in the text.” (hold your horses, put the cart before the horse, a wild goose chase, brings home the bacon, make hay while the sun shines). Then the questions.
  6. Speaking (10 min). Questions 1 and 4 are the core. Push for a range of comparison forms, not just better.
  7. Sort and wrap-up (3 min). Exercise 7 Part A together; Part B at home.

Key vocabulary: notes

Pronunciation

  • rural /ˈrʊərəl/: one of the hardest words in English for many students. Practise it slowly: ROO-rul. If it really doesn't work, in the country is a fine alternative.
  • urban /ˈɜːbən/: the same vowel as bird and work, not ar.
  • commute /kəˈmjuːt/: stress on the second syllable, for both the noun and the verb.
  • isolated /ˈaɪsəleɪtɪd/: the first syllable is eye; the -ed is an extra syllable after t.
  • Comparatives: the -er and than are weak: quieter than /ˈkwaɪətə ðən/. Students often stress than.
  • further /ˈfɜːðə/: the th is voiced, as in this.

Idioms, register and common mistakes

  • Informal idioms: hold your horses, bring home the bacon and till the cows come home are informal: fine with friends and colleagues, not in a formal email. Get your ducks in a row and the pecking order are common in work talk. The cream of the crop, a wild goose chase and putting the cart before the horse are neutral and fine almost anywhere.
  • Idioms are fixed. Students change small words: till the cows go home, count your eggs, the cream of the milk. Praise the meaning, then correct the form.
  • more cheaper, more better: the most common comparative error. One way only: -er or more.
  • not as ... than: a mix of two patterns. It's not as busy as or less busy than.
  • very + comparative: very cheaper is wrong. Use much, a lot, a bit, far: much cheaper.
  • the country / a country / the countryside: I live in the country = outside towns. Canada is a big country = a nation. Spanish campo, French campagne, Italian campagna = the country(side), not camp or campaign.
  • Other idioms: the black sheep and like a bull in a china shop are farm idioms too; they are taught in the Advanced English similes and metaphors units, so they are left out here.

Adapting the lesson

ElementB1B2
VocabularyFocus on five idioms (make hay while the sun shines, don't count your chickens, hold your horses, a wild goose chase, bring home the bacon) and the regular comparative rules.All ten idioms, plus the black sheep and like a bull in a china shop; add far / slightly / considerably + comparative, the + comparative, the + comparative (the further from the city, the cheaper the houses).
SpeakingCompare two places they know with three or four sentences each.A mini-debate: the student argues for city life, then for country life. Then a short story using two idioms naturally.

Speaking prompts and follow-ups

  • Country or city? Follow up: “What would you miss most?” “Is it better for children? For older people?” Listen for more cheaper and not as ... than.
  • Then and now. Follow up: “Is your commute longer or shorter now?” “Which place has the best food?” Good for superlatives.
  • Idioms in your language. Many languages have a version of don't count your chickens (Spanish no vendas la piel del oso antes de cazarlo, French il ne faut pas vendre la peau de l'ours). Ask: “Which animal is in your version?”
  • A wild goose chase story. Tell yours first. Follow up: “What happened in the end?” “What did you learn?” Coach the student to put the idiom at the end as a summary: It was a real wild goose chase.

Answers

  • Exercise 1: make hay while the sun shines = use a good chance while you have it; don't count your chickens = don't be sure of a good result too early; till the cows come home = for a very long time; put the cart before the horse = do things in the wrong order; bring home the bacon = earn the money for the family; the cream of the crop = the best of a group; a wild goose chase = a long search that brings no result; hold your horses = wait, slow down.
  • Exercise 2: 1 quieter, 2 much more expensive, 3 as, 4 most peaceful, 5 worse, 6 longer, 7 a lot, 8 further.
  • Exercise 3: 1 countryside, 2 commute, 3 neighbours, 4 wildlife, 5 urban, 6 rural, 7 isolated, 8 community.
  • Exercise 4: 1 The village is much quieter than the city. 2 Life on a farm is not as easy as people think. 3 Houses in the country are a lot cheaper. 4 This is the most beautiful view in the valley. 5 My commute is longer than it was before.
  • Exercise 5: 1 toronto, 2 hens, 3 thirty, 4 husband, 5 community.
  • Exercise 7: Part A: -er / -ier: quiet, busy, safe. more: expensive, peaceful, crowded, isolated. irregular: good, bad, far. Part B: 1 The village is cheaper than Toronto. 2 Don't count your chickens before they hatch. 3 The farm is not as busy as the city. 4 No mistake. 5 We could talk about farming until the cows come home.
