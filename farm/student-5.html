What this lesson is for

You will be able to

  • describe a farmer's daily routine, or your own routine with a pet
  • say how often things happen on a farm
  • talk about how animals should be looked after

You will know

  • feed, water, milk, groom, shear, muck out
  • trough, hay, straw, milking parlour
  • let out, bring in, round up, check on

You will understand

  • She always feeds / They are always hungry
  • let them out (not let out them)

Common mistakes to watch for

  • She feeds always the calves first. She always feeds the calves first.
  • The cows always are hungry in the morning. The cows are always hungry in the morning.
  • Can you let out them? Can you let them out?
  • The farmer milks the cows two times in a day. The farmer milks the cows twice a day.
  • We put clean hay on the floor for them to sleep on. We put clean straw on the floor. (hay is food)
  • The cow gave birth to a calf yesterday night. The cow gave birth to a calf last night. / The cow calved last night.

Warm-up

  • Do you have a pet, or did you have one as a child? Who looked after it?
  • What do you think a dairy farmer does every day? What time does the day start?
  • Which animal job would you like to try: milking a cow, feeding lambs or brushing a horse? Which would you hate?

Key vocabulary

Daily jobs

VerbMeaningExample
feedgive food to an animal (fed, fed)We feed the pigs at seven.
watergive water to an animal or plantDon't forget to water the horses.
milktake milk from a cow, goat or sheepThe cows are milked twice a day.
grazeeat grass in a fieldThe sheep graze on the hills all summer.
groombrush and clean an animal's coatShe grooms her horse after every ride.
shearcut the wool off a sheep (sheared, shorn)The sheep are shorn in June.
muck outclean the dirty straw out of a stable or shedI muck out the stable every morning.

Phrasal verbs for moving animals

These phrasal verbs can be split. With a noun, both orders are fine: let the hens out or let out the hens. With a pronoun (it, them), the pronoun must go in the middle: let them out, never let out them.

Phrasal verbMeaningExample
let outopen the door so animals can go outsideLet the hens out at eight. Let them out at eight.
bring inbring animals inside, into a buildingBring the sheep in before the storm.
round upbring animals together into one group or placeThe dog rounds them up in ten minutes.
shut inclose the door so animals stay insideShut the chickens in at night, because of the foxes.
check ongo and see if an animal is OK (not separable)I check on the new calf every two hours.

Things, people and places

WordMeaning
trougha long open container for animals' food or water
hay / strawhay = dried grass, food; straw = dry stems, bedding
feedfood for animals (uncountable): a bag of feed
milking parlourthe building where cows are milked (US parlor)
sheepdoga dog trained to move sheep
vetan animal doctor (short for veterinarian)
lambing seasonthe weeks in spring when the lambs are born
calve, lamb, foalverbs: give birth to a calf, a lamb, a foal: The cow calved last night.

How often?

Adverbs of frequency go before the main verb (She always feeds them) but after be (They are always hungry). Longer phrases go at the end: twice a day, every morning, once a week.

AdverbExample
always (100%)The cows always come to the gate at milking time.
usually / normallyWe usually shear the sheep in June.
oftenThe vet often visits in lambing season.
sometimesThe goats sometimes escape.
rarely / hardly everThe horses are rarely ill.
never (0%)A dairy farmer never has a day off.

Say it right: trough rhymes with off (/trɒf/); shear sounds like sheer; shorn rhymes with born; calve has a silent l; muck rhymes with duck.

My week as a farm volunteer: Monday

I arrived at Hillside Farm in Nova Scotia last night, and today I started work at half past five! The cows are always milked first. They walk into the milking parlour on their own, because they know the routine. While Jenna milked them, I fed the calves with big bottles of warm milk. They pushed me so hard that I nearly fell over.

After breakfast we let the cows out into the field to graze. Then I mucked out the calf shed. It's a dirty job, but the clean straw smells lovely. Jenna checks the water troughs twice a day, because a cow can drink up to a hundred litres of water on a hot day.

In the afternoon the vet came to see a cow that was limping. Luckily it was only a small stone in her foot.

Exercise 7: Phrasal verbs and mistakes

Part A: Out, in or up?

Watch out

  • out: from inside to outside, or cleaning something completely: let the hens out, muck out the shed
  • in: from outside to inside: bring the sheep in, shut the hens in
  • up: together, or completely: round up the cattle, fill up the trough
