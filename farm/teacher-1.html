Teacher Notes 1: Farm Animals and Their Young

Open the student page

Purpose: give general learners the core words for farm animals: the animal, the male, the female, the young and the group (bull, cow, calf, a herd), the sounds they make, and the plural traps (sheep, geese, oxen, calves, cattle, livestock). It is the base for the rest of the Farm English lessons.

Lesson time: 50 minutes, plus a few exercises to finish at home.

Level: B1 to B2. Before the lesson: find three or four photos of farm animals with their young (a ewe and lambs, a sow and piglets, a hen and chicks). Or ask the student to bring a photo of a farm or farm animal from their own country.

Lesson plan (50 minutes)

  1. Warm-up (5 min). The three warm-up questions. Write down every animal the student already knows in English, and note any they say in their own language.
  2. Key vocabulary (10 min). Go through the first table by animal (across), then test by column: “What's a female pig?” “What's a group of cows?” Use your photos. Then the tricky plurals, then the sounds (students usually enjoy making them).
  3. Exercises 1 and 2 (8 min). Matching, then the plurals and group words. In Exercise 2, ask why after each answer: sheep is the same, cattle is plural.
  4. Exercise 3 or 4 (5 min). Do one together and set the other for home.
  5. Reading (8 min). The farm blog. First ask: “How many kinds of animal does Marta have?” (five: cows, sheep, hens with a rooster, pigs, and the calf and lambs as babies). Then the questions.
  6. Speaking (10 min). Questions 1 and 2 are the core. Ask follow-ups that need the plurals: “How many sheep?” “Were there any geese?”
  7. Plurals and wrap-up (4 min). Exercise 7 Part A together; Part B at home.

Key vocabulary: notes

Pronunciation

  • sheep /ʃiːp/ vs ship /ʃɪp/: the long vowel matters. Many learners say ship.
  • calf /kɑːf/ (North American /kæf/): the l is silent, as in half. calves /kɑːvz/.
  • lamb /læm/: the b is silent, as in climb and thumb.
  • ewe /juː/: exactly like you. Students often try to say every letter.
  • sow (a female pig) /saʊ/ rhymes with cow; sow (to plant seeds, Lesson 4) /səʊ/ rhymes with go.
  • goose /ɡuːs/, geese /ɡiːs/: an s sound, not z. foal /fəʊl/, mare /meə/.

False friends and common mistakes

  • cattle is plural only: the cattle are, forty head of cattle (farmers count cattle as head). Never a cattle or cattles. For one animal, say a cow, a bull or a steer.
  • livestock: all the farm animals together. No -s, no a. You may hear both livestock is and livestock are; both are fine.
  • cow is female. In everyday speech people call any cattle “cows”, but a male is a bull. A young male raised for meat is a steer (B2).
  • chicken is the general word (and the meat). Hen = female, rooster = male. In the UK cockerel is also common; in North America say rooster, because cock is also slang.
  • pig / pork, cow / beef, sheep / lamb, mutton: live animal vs meat. Lesson 6 covers this; just flag it if the student says porks.
  • Spanish ganado, French bétail, German Vieh can mean cattle or livestock. Check which one the student means: cattle are only cows and bulls.
  • kid = a young goat, and also an informal word for a child. Students find this funny; it helps them remember it.

Adapting the lesson

ElementB1B2
VocabularyFocus on cows, sheep, pigs, horses and chickens: male, female, young. Groups: herd, flock. Plurals: sheep, geese, calves, cattle.All animals and groups; add steer, heifer (a young cow that has not had a calf), gaggle, a pair of oxen, forty head of cattle.
SpeakingDescribe a farm visit or photo: what animals, how many, what they are doing.Compare farm animals in two countries, or explain why some animals are special in their culture (e.g. cows in India, horses in Mongolia).

Speaking prompts and follow-ups

  • Have you visited a farm? Follow up: “How many cows were there?” “Were there any babies?” “What were the animals doing?” Listen for sheeps and the cattle is.
  • Farm animals in your country. Follow up: “Which animal is the most important for farmers there?” “Do people eat it, use its milk or use it for work?”
  • Animal sounds in your language. A light, fun task. Follow up: “What does a rooster say in your language?” Compare with cock-a-doodle-doo.
  • Hens or a goat at home? Good for reasons: because, so, but. Follow up: “What would you need?” “What would your neighbours say?”

Answers

  • Exercise 1: calf = a young cow; lamb = a young sheep; piglet = a young pig; foal = a young horse; kid = a young goat; chick = a young chicken; duckling = a young duck; gosling = a young goose.
  • Exercise 2: 1 sheep, 2 flock, 3 are, 4 litter, 5 stallion, 6 geese, 7 rooster, 8 livestock.
  • Exercise 3: 1 ewe, 2 bull, 3 sow, 4 mare, 5 calves, 6 oxen, 7 mice, 8 herd.
  • Exercise 4: 1 The lambs are born in early spring. 2 How many sheep does your uncle have? 3 The cattle are coming back from the field. 4 Our hens lay about twenty eggs a day. 5 A young goat is called a kid.
  • Exercise 5: 1 sixty, 2 march, 3 days, 4 rooster, 5 piglets.
  • Exercise 7: Part A: the same: sheep, deer, bison. changes: goose, mouse, ox, calf. add -s: hen, goat, horse. Part B: 1 We saw three sheep in the field. 2 The cattle are eating grass by the river. 3 No mistake. 4 The farmer has a herd of cows. 5 Two calves were born last night.
