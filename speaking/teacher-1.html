Teacher Notes 1: Naming the Freeze

Open the student page

Purpose: help the student see freezing as a normal, predictable reaction, identify their own triggers, and end the lesson with one successful speaking experience.

Lesson time: 50 minutes, then daily homework.

Before the lesson: send the student a short message asking them to describe one recent moment when they froze speaking English. Their answer gives you real material for this lesson and for Lesson 7.

Why adult learners freeze

  • Adults are used to being competent. Speaking a new language makes them feel less capable than they are, and that feeling is uncomfortable.
  • Many learners try to build a perfect sentence in their head before speaking. Translating and checking grammar at the same time overloads working memory, and the words disappear.
  • Past experiences matter: being laughed at, corrected in front of others, or not understood on the phone can create a strong fear response in similar situations.

Share the second point with the student. Adults relax when they learn the freeze has a cause and is not a sign that they are bad at English.

Four causes behind the freeze

Use these to explain the freeze to the student in plain words. Most adults feel relief when they hear it is a normal brain reaction, not a lack of intelligence.

  • The panic loop. High anxiety shuts down working memory. The words are in the student's head, but stress blocks the way to them. This is what teachers call the affective filter.
  • Mental translation. Translating from their own language and back again overloads processing speed. Short, memorised chunks skip the translation step.
  • Perfectionism. Fear of a mistake, of sounding foolish or of their accent makes students stay silent rather than risk an imperfect sentence.
  • Recall versus recognition. Reading and worksheets use recognition: the word is in front of them. Speaking needs unprompted recall, under time pressure. A student can score well on a worksheet and still freeze.

Every grammar and vocabulary worksheet in your ESL folder now has a “Say it until it's automatic” section for the student and an 8-minute “Freeze-proofing this lesson” routine in the teacher notes. They work on the fourth cause directly, and the rescue phrases from Lesson 2 handle the first.

How to run it

  1. Easy warm-up (5 min). Ask three questions the student can answer in a word or two: “Where are you right now? What did you eat today? What is the weather like?” Keep it light.
  2. Talk about the freeze (10 min). Tell them almost every language learner freezes, including you in a language you have learned. Ask: “When does it happen to you? What do you feel? What do you think?” Lower levels can tick options in Part A of their worksheet.
  3. Freeze map (10 min). Together, rate the situations in Part B from 1 (easy) to 5 (very hard). Ask what makes the hard ones hard: speed, strangers, the phone, being judged, not knowing a word.
  4. Low-stakes speaking (15 min). Student picks an easy topic (rated 1 or 2) and speaks for 30 seconds (A2 to B1) or 1 minute (B1 to B2). Do not correct. Then repeat the same topic for the same time. Ask: “Which one felt easier?”
  5. Reframe (7 min). “Native speakers pause, restart and forget words all the time. The difference is they keep going.” Preview Lesson 2: phrases for exactly that.
  6. Wrap-up (3 min). Student says one thing they did well today. Set the homework in Part C.

Correction tips

  • Do not correct anything during this lesson. The only goal is a positive speaking experience.
  • Speak a little slower and warmer than usual. Your pace sets theirs.
  • Keep a copy of the student's freeze map ratings. You will compare them in Lesson 7.

If the student asks...

  • “Will I ever stop freezing?” It becomes less frequent and shorter. The bigger change is that a freeze stops being a disaster, because you know what to do next.
  • “Why can I write well but not speak?” Writing gives you time to plan and check. Speaking happens in real time. This course is about managing that time.
  • “Is my accent the problem?” Almost never. Listeners adapt to accents quickly. Hesitation and silence cause more misunderstanding than accent does.

On the web page

The student page keeps Parts A, B and C and adds three short exercises. Nothing on the page is timed: the speaking turn stays with you, and you keep the time. Part A ticks, the freeze map ratings and the “which time felt easier?” box are not marked, but they arrive with “Send my answers to Bill”, so you have the freeze map for Lesson 7.

The page explains the four causes in student words: stress (the panic loop), translating (mental translation), being perfect (perfectionism) and knowing vs saying (recall versus recognition).

  • Exercise 1, Match the reasons (tap to match): Stress = the word is in my head, but I am too nervous to find it. Translating = I build the sentence in my language first. Being perfect = I am afraid of a mistake, so I say nothing. Knowing vs saying = I know the word when I see it, but can't say it when I need it.
  • Exercise 2, Which reason is it? (tap the reason, 7 short stories): 1 stress, 2 translating, 3 being perfect, 4 knowing vs saying, 5 being perfect, 6 stress, 7 translating.
  • Exercise 3, Describe it without its name (spoken): the student describes an everyday object aloud, without naming it, and you guess. Use it with the object homework in Part C.
