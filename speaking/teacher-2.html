Teacher Notes 2: Rescue Phrases

Open the student page

Purpose: give the student a small personal kit of phrases for the four moments that cause most freezes: I don't understand, I can't find the word, I need a moment, and I made a mistake.

Lesson time: 50 minutes, then daily homework.

Why rescue phrases work

  • A freeze usually starts with one small problem: a missed word, a forgotten noun, an error. Silence turns that small problem into a big one.
  • A rescue phrase fills the silence and tells the listener what is happening. The conversation continues, and the listener usually helps.
  • Because the phrases are fixed and practised, they come out automatically, even when the student is stressed.

The word-finding phrases (“It's like a...”, “It's the thing you use to...”) are the most powerful for freezers. Students who believe they need the exact word discover they can describe it instead.

How to run it

  1. Homework check (5 min). Ask the student to say one of their three daily sentences. Praise the habit, not the grammar.
  2. Present the phrases (10 min). Go through the four groups on the worksheet. Model each phrase with natural intonation. Student repeats.
  3. Choose your kit (5 min). Student picks one favourite phrase from each group and writes it in Part E. Owning the choice matters.
  4. Rescue drill (15 min). Ask questions on easy topics, but deliberately create problems: speak a little fast, use one unfamiliar word, ask something that needs thinking time. Student must respond with a rescue phrase each time. Keep it playful.
  5. Mini-conversation (10 min). A 5-minute chat on a topic the student chooses. Your only job is to count every rescue phrase they use.
  6. Feedback and wrap-up (5 min). Tell them the count: “You rescued yourself four times.” Give one or two language corrections at most. Set the homework.

Correction tips

  • Never correct a rescue phrase in the moment, even if it is slightly wrong. Using it at all is the win.
  • Use a hand signal on camera (an open palm) to mean “use a rescue phrase” when the student goes silent. It prompts without interrupting.
  • When they describe a word instead of naming it, give them the word afterwards, then move on.

If the student asks...

  • “Isn't it rude to ask people to repeat?” No. Native speakers ask each other to repeat all the time. It shows you are listening and want to understand.
  • “Won't fillers like ‘Let me think’ sound unnatural?” They sound very natural. Silence sounds less natural than a short phrase.
  • “What if I use the phrase and still can't answer?” Then say so simply: “Sorry, I'm not sure how to explain that. Can I try another way?” That is also a rescue.
