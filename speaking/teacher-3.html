Teacher Notes 3: Thinking Time

Open the student page

Purpose: teach the student to buy thinking time with fillers and easy sentence starts, and let them feel the difference between speaking to be perfect and speaking to be understood.

Lesson time: 50 minutes, then homework.

Why fluency comes before accuracy here

  • Nervous speakers usually know more English than they can use. Their accuracy is often fine; their flow is the problem.
  • Trying to plan a complete, correct sentence before speaking is the main cause of long pauses. Starting with a fixed phrase gets the sentence moving.
  • Repeating a task removes the surprise. The second and third attempts are smoother, and the student can feel the improvement.

Tell the student directly: fillers and sentence starters are tools fluent speakers use constantly. They are not mistakes.

How to run it

  1. Kit check (5 min). Student says their four rescue phrases from memory. Then ask a slightly tricky question so they use one naturally.
  2. Fillers and easy starts (10 min). Present Parts A and B. Model each with natural rhythm. Student repeats, then answers two quick questions using one from each part.
  3. 4-3-2 talk (15 min). Student chooses a topic and speaks about it for 4 minutes, then the same topic for 3 minutes, then 2 minutes. Do not correct between rounds. After round three, ask: “What changed?” Usually: faster, smoother, fewer pauses.
  4. Simple is fine (10 min). Work through Part C. Then give two prompts that tempt complex sentences (for example, “Explain why you started learning English”). Coach the student to break the answer into short sentences.
  5. Error review (7 min). Now share the errors you noted during the lesson. Student tries to fix each one first.
  6. Wrap-up (3 min). Student rates their confidence from 1 to 10 in Part D. Note it. Preview Lesson 4: a simple three-step formula for giving opinions. Ask them to start thinking about which real situation they want to practise in Lesson 7.

Correction tips

  • Resist correcting during the 4-3-2 activity. The point is flow, and interruptions undo it.
  • Write errors in the Preply chat or a private note as they happen, so you can review them at the end without relying on memory.
  • If a student over-uses one filler (“um” every few words), point it out kindly and suggest swapping some for “Well...” or “Let me see...”.

If the student asks...

  • “Won't short sentences make me sound simple?” Clear sounds confident. Many skilled speakers use short sentences on purpose. You can add complexity later, once the flow is there.
  • “Why are we repeating the same topic three times?” Repetition is how speaking becomes automatic. Musicians and athletes practise the same thing many times too.
  • “Is ‘um’ bad English?” No. Every English speaker uses it. It only becomes a problem if it replaces most of what you say.

Exam link

In Aptis and Cambridge speaking tests, long silences lose more marks than small grammar errors. Fillers and easy starts help students keep talking for the full time.

On the web page

The student page keeps Parts A to E and adds four exercises. There is no clock on the page: the 4-3-2 talk is described as a speaking task where you keep the time. The confidence rating, the “what changed?” box, the homework boxes and the Lesson 7 question are not marked, but they arrive with “Send my answers to Bill”.

  • Exercise 1, Filler or easy start? (sorting, 12 items): the six fillers from Part A and the six easy starts from Part B. Actually... goes with the fillers, as on the worksheet.
  • Exercise 2, Which is easiest to say? (tap the answer, 5 questions): each question has a long, complex answer, a short clear answer and a too-short answer. Answers: 1 For my job. My company works with Canada. So I need English. 2 On Saturday I cleaned the house. Then I visited my sister. She lives near the lake. 3 Yes, mostly. Some days are stressful. But I enjoy it. 4 It's quite small. It's very green. People are friendly. 5 Sorry, the bus was late. I waited twenty minutes.
  • Exercise 3, Make it short (word order, 4 items): 1 I like my job. Every day is different. 2 My city is often cold. But I enjoy walking there. 3 The bus didn't come. So I was late this morning. 4 The shop was closed on Sunday. I didn't know.
  • Exercise 4, Your turn to make it short (Claude checks each answer): three long sentences to break into two to four short ones. This matches step 4 of your lesson; you can use the same sentences as your “tempting” prompts.
