Teacher Notes 1: Welcoming Tourists

Open the student page

Purpose: give students the language to welcome a visitor (as a receptionist, café or shop worker, guide, or simply a friendly local) and, above all, to ask questions correctly: closed questions (yes/no and either/or) with short answers, and open wh- questions, with the helper verb before the subject.

Lesson time: 50 minutes, plus a few exercises to finish at home.

Level: B1 to B2. Before the lesson: ask the student if they work (or would like to work) with tourists, and where: a hotel, a restaurant, a shop, a tour company. Print or prepare the role-play cards below, and think of one or two real places in the student's town for the speaking tasks.

Lesson plan (50 minutes)

  1. Warm-up (5 min). The three warm-up questions. Write down any questions the student says, mistakes included; you will come back to them at the end.
  2. Key vocabulary (10 min). The welcome phrases first, then the question table. Ask the student to give you one more example of each type. Drill the short answers quickly: you ask, they answer (Are you tired? Yes, I am.). Model the intonation (see below).
  3. Exercises 1 and 2 (8 min). Matching, then the helper verbs. After each item in Exercise 2, ask: “Why are and not do?” The rule: be and -ing forms take are/is; normal verbs take do/does/did.
  4. Exercise 3 or 4 (5 min). Do one together and set the other for home.
  5. Reading (8 min). The hostel conversation. Before the questions, ask the student to find Priya's questions and say which are closed and which are open (closed: Do you have a reservation? Is this your first time in Toronto? Would you like a map?; open: How was your journey? What would you like to do while you're here?). Then read it aloud together, the student as Priya.
  6. Speaking (10 min). Role-play with the cards. First round: closed questions only. Second round: open questions only. Ask the student which round felt more like a real conversation.
  7. Closed, open and wrap-up (4 min). Exercise 7 Part A together. Go back to the questions you wrote down in the warm-up and let the student correct them. Part B at home.

Key vocabulary: notes

Pronunciation

  • Intonation: yes/no questions usually rise at the end (Is this your first visit? ↗); wh- questions usually fall (Where are you from? ↘). Either/or questions rise, then fall: Tea ↗ or coffee ↘? A rising either/or sounds like a yes/no question (“tea or coffee or something else?”).
  • Weak forms: in fast speech do you becomes /djə/ or /dʒə/, are you /əjə/, would you /ˈwʊdʒu/, does /dəz/. Students may not hear the helper verb at all; slow down first, then model the natural speed.
  • journey /ˈdʒɜːni/: the first sound is /dʒ/ as in jam, and the vowel is like her, not your.
  • welcome /ˈwelkəm/: stress on the first syllable. reservation /ˌrezəˈveɪʃən/: stress on VA.
  • Short answers: the helper verb is stressed and strong in short answers: Yes, I am. /æm/, not /əm/. Negatives: isn't, aren't, don't, doesn't, didn't, with a clear /t/ at the end or a short stop.

Common mistakes and first-language notes

  • Statement order with a rising voice: Spanish, Italian and Portuguese speakers often make questions with intonation only: You are tired? In informal English this is possible to check something you think you heard (You're from Edinburgh?), but at work use the full form: Are you tired?
  • Chinese and Vietnamese speakers often keep statement order and add a question word at the end (You are from where?), because their languages use question particles. Drill the pattern: question word + helper + subject + verb.
  • Russian, Polish and Ukrainian speakers have no helper verbs in questions, so they often drop do: Where you live? German and French speakers may put the main verb first: Like you coffee? Both need do: Where do you live? Do you like coffee?
  • Double past: Did you enjoyed? Remind the student that did carries the past, so the main verb goes back to the base form.
  • Wrong short answer: Are you hungry? Yes, I do. The short answer repeats the helper verb of the question: are → am, do → do, can → can.
  • Would you like vs Do you like: Would you like a coffee? is an offer (now). Do you like coffee? asks about general taste. Students often offer things with Do you like ...?
  • Too direct: What's your name? or Your passport? can sound abrupt at a hotel desk. Softer: Can I have your name, please? Could I see your passport, please?
  • Register with customers: learners often use correct but blunt questions (What do you want? Do you want a bag?). Practise the polite forms in the “Customer service” table: Would you like ...? Could I have ...? I'm afraid .... In role-plays, ask the student to repeat a blunt question politely.

Speaking prompts and follow-ups

  • Greet a visitor and ask three closed questions. Answer with short answers that test them: “No, I'm not.” “Yes, we did.” Listen for the helper verb before the subject, and for a rising voice at the end.
  • Ask three open questions. Give longer answers so the student has to listen and follow up: “Oh, Edinburgh! What's it like?” Afterwards, ask which questions kept the conversation going.
  • Helping a lost tourist. Follow up as the tourist: “Is it far?” “Can I walk?” “How long does it take?” The student practises giving information: It's a ten-minute walk. It's open until six.
  • A place tourists love. Ask open questions as a curious visitor: “What can I do there?” “When is the best time to go?” “How much does it cost?” Then let the student ask you about a place in your town.
