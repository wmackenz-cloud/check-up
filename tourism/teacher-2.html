Teacher Notes 2: Being a Tourist

Open the student page

Purpose: give learners the questions they need as tourists: directions, tickets, prices, times and opening hours. The grammar focus is question forms: closed questions (yes/no with the helper verb first, and either/or), open wh- questions (How do I get to ...? How much ...? How long ...? Which platform ...?) and polite indirect questions for strangers (Could you tell me where the station is? Do you know if ...?), plus checking that you understood.

Lesson time: 50 minutes, plus a few exercises to finish at home.

Level: B1 to B2. Before the lesson: choose a city the student would like to visit (or their own city) and have a map of it ready to share on screen. Print or prepare the role-play cards below.

Lesson plan (50 minutes)

  1. Warm-up (5 min). The three warm-up questions. Note every question the student makes and the word order they use; you will come back to these.
  2. Key vocabulary (10 min). Travel words first, then the question table. Write one question of each kind on the board and ask: “Can you answer it with yes or no?” That is the closed/open test. Then the directions phrases with the map.
  3. Exercises 1 and 2 (8 min). Matching, then the forms. In Exercise 2, ask the student to say each question aloud with the right intonation (up for closed, down for open).
  4. Exercise 3 or 4 (5 min). Do one together and set the other for home.
  5. Reading (8 min). The tourist office conversation. First ask: “Find two closed questions and two open questions in the text.” Then the five questions. Point out Ana's checking question: So we turn left at the hotel?
  6. Speaking (10 min). Role-plays with the cards below. You are the local or the ticket seller. Give directions with two or three steps so the student has to check (So I ...?).
  7. Question types and wrap-up (4 min). Exercise 7 Part A together; Part B at home.

Key vocabulary: notes

Pronunciation

  • Intonation: closed questions usually rise at the end (Can I pay by card? ↑), open questions usually fall (Where's the station? ↓). A rising open question can sound surprised or impatient.
  • Could you /ˈkʊdʒuː/: in fast speech it sounds like coojoo. Do you know often sounds like d'you know /djə ˈnəʊ/.
  • Excuse me /ɪkˈskjuːz mi/: stress on -cuse. The noun an excuse ends in /s/, the verb in /z/.
  • souvenir /ˌsuːvəˈnɪə/ (North American /ˌsuːvəˈnɪr/): stress at the end.
  • queue /kjuː/: like the letter Q; the extra letters are silent. admission /ədˈmɪʃən/: stress on -mi-.
  • What time does it: does it links to /dʌzɪt/. Students who say every word separately sound slower and more formal than they need to.

False friends and common mistakes

  • Missing helper verb: What time the museum opens? Many languages make questions with intonation only. In English, a present simple question needs do / does: What time does the museum open? The -s moves to does.
  • Inversion after Could you tell me: Could you tell me where is the station? is the most common error at B1 and B2, even for strong students. The question is already in Could you tell me; the rest is normal order.
  • Do you know is there ...? For yes/no questions inside a polite opener, use if or whether: Do you know if there's a bus?
  • How much costs it? (Spanish ¿Cuánto cuesta?, French Combien ça coûte?). Teach How much is it? as a fixed phrase, and How much does it cost? for B2.
  • Subject questions (B2): Which bus goes to the airport? has no does, because which bus is the subject. Compare Which bus do I take? Mention only if the student is ready.
  • Too direct: Where is the toilet? is fine at a counter, but to a stranger in the street start with Excuse me or Sorry to bother you, and use Could you tell me ...?
  • Short answers: Does this bus go to the airport? Yes, it does. Not Yes, it goes. The short answer repeats the helper verb.

Speaking prompts and follow-ups

  • Plan a day at the tourist office. Follow up with short answers so the student must ask more: “Yes, there is.” (“Where is it? How much is it?”). Count closed and open questions.
  • Asking the way. Give directions with three steps. Listen for a checking question (So I go ...?) and for Could you tell me where ... is? with normal order.
  • Buying a ticket. Follow up as the ticket seller: “Single or return?” “Today or tomorrow?” “Card or cash?” These either/or questions show how closed questions work.
  • A time you asked for help on holiday. Past questions: “What did you ask?” “Did they understand you?” “What would you say now?”
