Teacher Notes 1: School Types and Stages

Open the student page

Purpose: give teachers the words to describe school systems and stages in both American and British English, so they can explain their own system and their own school to colleagues, parents and interview panels abroad (elementary / primary, high school / secondary school, public / state school, grade / Year), and the small words that go with them (teach at, be in Year 9, go to school, eleven-year-olds).

Lesson time: 50 minutes, plus homework.

Level: B1 to B2. Before the lesson: ask the student which country's system they work in and what kind of school (or university) they teach at. Ask if they need American English, British English or both: for example, an international school, a job abroad or online students from one country. Let that decide which column you push in speaking.

Lesson plan (50 minutes)

  1. Warm-up (5 min). The three warm-up questions. Listen for literal translations of school names (colegio, collège, liceum, instituto) and note them.
  2. Key vocabulary (12 min). Draw a timeline on the screen from age 3 to 18. Write the US words above the line and the UK words below it. Then add the student's own system in a third row.
  3. Exercises 1 and 2 (7 min). US or UK, then the partners. Ask for a reason for each chip, not only the column.
  4. Language point and Exercise 3 (6 min). Focus on at / in, no article with go to school, and eleven-year-olds.
  5. Reading (8 min). Helen's email. Ask the student to underline every UK school word, then say the US word for each.
  6. Speaking (9 min). Questions 1 and 4 are the core. The student explains their timeline; then you play the interview panel.
  7. Prepositions and wrap-up (3 min). Exercise 8 Part A together. Exercises 4, 5, 8 Part B and the homework at home.

Key vocabulary: notes

Pronunciation

  • kindergarten /ˈkɪndəɡɑːtn/ (UK), /ˈkɪndərɡɑːrtn/ (US): spelled and said with t, not d (not kindergarden).
  • secondary /ˈsekəndri/ (UK), /ˈsekənderi/ (US) and primary /ˈpraɪməri/ (UK), /ˈpraɪmeri/ (US): stress on the first syllable. Americans say the -ary more fully.
  • compulsory /kəmˈpʌlsəri/: stress on PUL.
  • authority /ɔːˈθɒrəti/: stress on THOR, and a clear /θ/.
  • university /ˌjuːnɪˈvɜːsəti/: starts with /j/, so a university. Many students say an university.
  • independent /ˌɪndɪˈpendənt/: stress on PEN.

Getting the US and UK pairs right

  • reception / kindergarten are not the same age: English reception is age 4 to 5, US kindergarten is 5 to 6. They are both the first year of school.
  • middle school: England mostly has no middle stage; Years 7 to 9 are part of secondary school (a few areas still have middle schools). Some US districts have junior high (grades 7 to 9) instead.
  • high school covers grades 9 to 12 (ages 14 to 18). The closest UK match is the second half of secondary school plus the sixth form.
  • academy / charter school is only a rough match: both are publicly funded and run independently, but the rules are different.
  • Canada (Ontario): JK and SK (junior and senior kindergarten), elementary school, high school (grades 9 to 12), school board, marks, washroom. Useful if the student has Canadian contacts.
  • grammar school: in England, a selective state secondary school (students pass an exam at 11). In the US it is an old word for elementary school.

False friends and common mistakes

  • college: Spanish colegio and Portuguese colégio mean a school (often private); French collège is a lower secondary school (ages 11 to 15); Italian collegio is a boarding school. Say school, or middle school / lower secondary school for collège.
  • lycée / liceo / liceum / instituto: say high school (US) or secondary school / sixth form (UK), not lyceum or institute.
  • gymnasium: Polish gimnazjum (the old lower secondary school) and German Gymnasium are schools. In English a gym is a sports hall.
  • high school vs higher education: Polish szkoła wyższa (literally “higher school”) and Italian scuola superiore confuse students. Szkoła wyższa is a university; scuola superiore is upper secondary school. English high school is always school, never university.
  • graduate: Spanish graduarse and Portuguese formar-se work for any level. In British English, graduate is for university; for school say leave school / finish school. In American English, graduate from high school is normal.
  • Portuguese (Brazil): ensino fundamental covers US elementary plus middle school (ages 6 to 14); ensino médio is high school (15 to 17). Faculdade is a university or college, not a faculty.
  • Vietnamese: tiểu học, trung học cơ sở, trung học phổ thông are primary, lower secondary and upper secondary. Students sometimes say “level 2 school” (from cấp 2): teach middle school / lower secondary. Watch the final /s/ in schools, students, years and missing articles: a state school.
  • I have 12 years (Spanish, Italian, French, Portuguese): say I am 12 or 12 years old. And 12-year-old students, never 12-years-old students.

Systems do not translate one to one. Teach the student to describe a stage by age and purpose first (a school for students aged 15 to 18 who are preparing for university), and then give the closest US or UK name.

Speaking prompts and follow-ups

  • Draw the system. Follow up: “At what age do students choose a type of school?” “Is there an exam at the end?”
  • Describe your school. Follow up: “Is it a state or a private school?” “What are the students like?” Push for students aged 12 to 15 and a school of 600 students.
  • Compare systems. Useful phrases: In my country ..., whereas in the UK ...; The closest thing to ... is ...
  • Interview question. Coach a one-minute answer: the type of school and the ages, what you teach, and one thing you are proud of there.

Notes on the exercises

  • Exercise 1: secondary school is a general word in American English too, but in everyday speech Americans say high school or middle school. Accept a discussion.
  • Exercise 2: the last pair is a verb, not a school. Use it to show that graduate is used differently.
  • Exercise 3: item 5 is the most common mistake in this lesson. Ask the student to make two sentences: a class of eleven-year-olds and an eleven-year-old student.
  • Exercise 4: each sentence says (US) or (UK) where it matters. Ask for the other variety's word after each answer.
  • Exercise 6: the reading is British. Ask the student to retell it for an American colleague using US words.
  • Exercise 8: Part A shows a real US / UK difference: at university (UK) and in college (US).

Answers

  • Exercise 1: US English: elementary school, kindergarten, middle school, high school, ninth grade, senior year. UK English: primary school, reception, sixth form, secondary school, Year 10, upper sixth.
  • Exercise 2: preschool = nursery school; elementary school = primary school; public school = state school; private school = independent school; school district = local authority; first grade = Year 2; senior year = Year 13; graduate from high school = leave school.
  • Exercise 3: 1 at, 2 in, 3 school, 4 college, 5 eleven-year-olds, 6 state, 7 grade, 8 graduate.
  • Exercise 4: 1 reception, 2 middle school, 3 sixth form, 4 kindergarten, 5 independent, 6 district, 7 compulsory, 8 boarding.
  • Exercise 5: 1 I teach English at a state secondary school. 2 My students are fourteen and fifteen years old. 3 High school in the US usually starts in ninth grade. 4 Children in England start primary school at four. 5 What age are the students at your school?
  • Exercise 6: 1 18, 2 government, 3 11, 4 sixth, 5 13. The writing task is checked by Claude.
  • Exercise 8: Part A: at: teach at a primary school, start school at the age of five, at the end of Year 11, study at university (UK). in: be in Year 9, be in ninth grade, stay in the sixth form. to: go to college (US), move up to secondary school, send their children to a private school. Part B: 1 My daughter goes to school by bus every day. 2 I teach in a private school for 12-year-old students. 3 My nephew is in Year 5 this year. 4 No mistake. 5 He finished high school in 2010 and went to college.
