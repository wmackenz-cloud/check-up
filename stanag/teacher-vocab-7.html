Teacher Notes 7: Maps, Terrain and Logistics

Open the student page

Purpose: give military and police students the language to read and say grid references, describe terrain and a route with directions and distances, and talk about supply and transport (convoy, resupply, supply point, rations, fuel, spare parts), with the uncountable nouns that cause most mistakes.

Lesson time: 60 minutes, plus homework.

Level: STANAG Level 1+ to 2, with a Level 3 extension (Speaking question 4 and the ideas below). Before the lesson: have a map ready to share on screen: a free online topographic map of the student's area, or of somewhere neutral such as the Dolomites or the Pyrenees. Keep the talk to everyday map reading and logistics, not tactics.

Lesson plan (60 minutes)

  1. Warm-up (5 min). The three warm-up questions. Ask the student to describe where they live in two sentences: you will hear the prepositions they need.
  2. Key vocabulary (12 min). Grid references first, with the shared map: you give a six-figure grid, the student finds it and says what is there. Then terrain (point to features on the map) and logistics.
  3. Exercises 1 and 2 (8 min). Sorting, then terrain features. Ask the student to find a ridge, a saddle and a spur on the map.
  4. Exercises 3 and 4 (8 min). Complete the sentences, then build the route sentences. Read the finished route aloud.
  5. Reading (9 min). “Moving the Supplies.” After the questions, ask: “Would you choose the same route? Why?”
  6. Speaking (12 min). Question 2 with the map: the student guides you from A to B while you follow on screen. Then swap. Question 3 practises the logistics words.
  7. Prepositions and articles (6 min). Part A together; Parts B and C and the homework at home.

Key vocabulary: notes

Pronunciation

  • route /ruːt/: British and international; /raʊt/ is common in Canada and the US. Both are fine; be consistent.
  • terrain /təˈreɪn/: stress on the second syllable.
  • contour /ˈkɒntʊə/: stress on the first syllable.
  • ridge /rɪdʒ/ and bridge /brɪdʒ/: short /ɪ/, final /dʒ/. Spanish speakers often say reech.
  • rations /ˈræʃnz/: British and military usage; /ˈreɪʃnz/ is also heard in North America.
  • valley /ˈvæli/: Spanish speakers often confuse /v/ and /b/: valley, not bally.
  • kilometre: /kɪˈlɒmɪtə/ or /ˈkɪləmiːtə/; both are standard.

False friends and common mistakes

  • equipments, fuels, ammunitions, informations: Spanish equipos, municiones, Italian munizioni, French munitions and Polish amunicja lead to plurals. In English these nouns are uncountable: a lot of equipment, some fuel.
  • carta / carte (Spanish, Italian, French) = map, not card or chart. A chart is a sea or air map, or a graph.
  • distant 3 km (Italian distante 3 km, Spanish a 3 km): say 3 km from or 3 km away. Not at 3 km from here.
  • arrive to (Spanish llegar a, Italian arrivare a): arrive at a point, arrive in a town.
  • sierra: Spanish sierra = a mountain range, not a single hill. In English, sierra is only in names (and the letter S).
  • material: Spanish and Italian material / materiale often means equipment or supplies. In English materials are what you build or make things with.
  • on the left / at the left: Polish na lewo and French à gauche lead to at the left. Say on the left.

Notes on the exercises

  • Exercise 1 (sort, 12 chips): quick. Use it to check the student knows easting.
  • Exercise 2 (match, 6 pairs): if possible, show each feature on a real map or a photo.
  • Exercise 3 (type, 8 items): in item 5, checkpoint on a route is a reporting point. In lesson 8 the same word means a place where vehicles are stopped. Point out the difference.
  • Exercise 4 (word order, 6 sentences): together they make a route. Ask the student to draw it.
  • Exercise 5 (reading): good for too + adjective (too deep) and comparatives (longer, shortest, fastest). The writing task is checked by Claude.
  • Exercise 7: Part A is prepositions of place and movement; Part B is articles with uncountable nouns and the Alps; Part C is uncountable nouns and arrive at.

Extension for Level 3

  • Speaking question 4: ask for a recommendation with conditions: Unless ..., Provided that ..., Even if ....
  • Add gradient, steep / gentle slope, dead ground, high ground, re-entrant, track, metalled road, bypass, bottleneck, and logistics words like lead time, shortfall, stockpile, consumption rate.
  • The student plans a resupply for a fictional camp and explains the choice of route, timings and loads, then answers your “what if” questions (What if the bridge is closed?).
  • Compare logistics problems in civilian life: a supermarket chain in winter, a ski resort, a building site in Toronto.

Answers

  • Exercise 1: Terrain: ridge, valley, ford, treeline. Map reading: grid reference, contour line, bearing, easting. Logistics: rations, fuel, spare parts, convoy.
  • Exercise 2: ridge = a long, narrow line of high ground; valley = low land between hills, often with a river; saddle = a low point between two hilltops; spur = high ground that goes out from a hill, like a finger; ford = a shallow place to cross a river; built-up area = a town or village with many buildings.
  • Exercise 3: 1 convoy, 2 contour lines, 3 built-up area, 4 bearing, 5 checkpoint, 6 supply point, 7 treeline, 8 resupply.
  • Exercise 4: 1 Follow the main road north for two kilometres. 2 Turn left at the crossroads after the church. 3 Cross the river at the ford. 4 The supply point is on the right. 5 Always read the easting before the northing. 6 Keep the treeline on your left.
  • Exercise 5: 1 kilometres, 2 parts, 3 deep, 4 saddle, 5 checkpoints; 6 open answer.
  • Exercise 7: Part A: 1 from, 2 at, 3 along, 4 through, 5 on, 6 over, 7 of, 8 of. Part B: 1 the, 2 a, 3 – (no article), 4 the, 5 an, 6 the, 7 – (no article), 8 a. Part C: 1 The checkpoont is in the left of the road. 2 We need more fuel for the trucks. 3 We need more equipment for the new camp. 4 We arrived at the supply point at noon.
  • Exercise 6 and homework: open answers. Go through them together in the next lesson.
