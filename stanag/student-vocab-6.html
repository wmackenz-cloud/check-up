What this lesson is for

Why this matters: orders and briefings follow the same structure in every NATO army, so if you know it, you can follow a briefing in English and give one yourself. Times and checking understanding are where most mistakes happen.

You will know

  • the five paragraphs of an order: Situation, Mission, Execution, Admin and logistics, Command and signal (SMEAC)
  • briefing words: warning order, intent, task, timings, H-hour
  • how to say times with the 24-hour clock: zero six hundred, fourteen thirty

You will understand

  • why the mission is one clear sentence: who, what, when, where and why
  • why asking people to confirm their task works better than “Is that clear?”

You will be able to

  • give and confirm simple orders
  • ask for clarification politely: Could you clarify the timings, please?

Common mistakes to watch for

  • asking “Is that clear?” Ask people to tell you their task: Team 2, can you tell me your task?
  • We move on 0600. It starts at 3 June. We move at 0600. It starts on 3 June. We leave in the morning.
  • Be ready until 1700. Be ready by 1700 (not later than). Stay until 1700 (up to that moment).
  • The captain briefed us of the mission. The captain briefed us on the mission. Also in charge of a team, in order to + verb.
  • arrive to the meeting point arrive at the meeting point; questions at the end of the briefing
  • Intent of this operation is... The intent of this operation is... We meet in the briefing room. (the with things that are unique or already known)
  • The H-hour is 0600. H-hour is 0600. Planning saves time. (No article for names like H-hour or general uncountable nouns.)
  • a hour, a excellent briefer an hour, an excellent briefer (an before a vowel sound)

Warm-up

  • How often do you give or receive briefings? Have you ever had one in English?
  • What makes a briefing clear and easy to follow?
  • Have you ever misunderstood an instruction? What happened?

Key vocabulary

Word or phraseMeaningExample
warning order (WARNO)a short, early message that tells units a task is coming, so they can start preparingThe warning order arrived at 1800, so we checked the vehicles that night.
five-paragraph orderthe full standard order, in five parts: Situation, Mission, Execution, Administration and logistics, Command and signal (SMEAC)The captain gave the five-paragraph order the next morning.
situationparagraph 1: the area, the weather, the ground, other units and groupsThe situation has changed since the floods.
missionparagraph 2: one clear sentence that says who, what, when, where and whyOur mission is to deliver tents to the camp by 1600.
executionparagraph 3: how the mission will be done: the intent, the tasks and the timingsThe execution paragraph is the longest.
administration and logistics (service support)paragraph 4: food, water, fuel, transport and medical supportUnder administration and logistics: rations for two days.
command and signalparagraph 5: who is in command, where the commander will be, call signs and frequenciesCommand and signal: the major is at the main gate.
intentwhat the commander wants to achieve, and why; it helps people decide when plans changeThe commander's intent is that the aid arrives safely.
taska specific job given to a person or a teamEach team has one task.
timingsthe list of times for each stage of the planThe timings are on the last slide.
H-hourthe time when an operation or activity startsH-hour is zero six hundred.
to brief / a briefingto give information and instructions to a group / the meeting where this happensMajor Rossi will brief the team leaders at 1400.
to confirmto check or say that something is correctCan I just confirm the meeting point?
to clarifyto make something clearer, or to ask for something to be explainedCould you clarify what you mean by phase two?

The 24-hour clock

  • 0600 = zero six hundred (also: oh six hundred) · 0945 = zero nine forty-five
  • 1200 = twelve hundred · 1430 = fourteen thirty · 2000 = twenty hundred
  • In orders you may hear hours: We move at fourteen thirty hours.
  • H minus 15 = fifteen minutes before H-hour; H plus 2 = two hours after.

Briefing language

To ...Say
startGood morning. I will now brief the mission. This briefing will cover ...
move onMoving on to the execution. Next, the timings.
check understandingAny questions? Team 2, can you tell me your task?
confirmJust to confirm: we move at zero six hundred? So, if I understand correctly, ...
ask for clarificationCould you clarify the timings, please? What do you mean by ...? Could you go over that again?

Say it right: BRIEFing (long ee), inTENT, exeCUtion, loGIStics, CLARify, H is aitch and the h in hour is silent: aitch-our.

Exercise 5: Reading

First Briefing in English

Lieutenant Marco Bianchi is an Italian officer at a multinational headquarters in Spain. Last month he gave his first briefing in English, to team leaders from five countries. The task was simple: after a flood, a convoy would deliver tents and medical supplies to a camp, and his platoon would escort it.

He prepared carefully. He used the five-paragraph format, wrote the mission statement in one sentence and put all the timings on one slide. “The hardest part was the times,” he says. “In Italian we say them differently, so I practised zero six hundred and fourteen thirty again and again.”

His commander gave him two pieces of advice. First, say every important time and place twice. Second, do not ask “Is that clear?”, because nobody wants to say no in front of the group. Instead, ask people to tell you their task. “At the end, I asked each team leader to confirm their task and their timings,” Marco explains. “One of them had the wrong meeting point. We fixed it in ten seconds.”
