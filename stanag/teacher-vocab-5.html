Teacher Notes 5: Radio and Communications

Open the student page

Purpose: give military and police students the core radio English they need on multinational exercises and missions: the NATO phonetic alphabet, numbers the radio way, standard procedure words (roger, wilco, over, out, say again, read back, correction, wait one), call signs, and the four-part structure of a short, clear message.

Lesson time: 60 minutes, plus homework.

Level: STANAG Level 1+ to 2, with a Level 3 extension (Speaking question 4 and the ideas below). Before the lesson: ask the student which call signs and procedure words their own service uses in their language. Many students already know the alphabet; the real work is speed, numbers and the procedure words.

Lesson plan (60 minutes)

  1. Warm-up (5 min). The three warm-up questions. Ask the student to spell their surname with the alphabet straight away: you will hear what needs work.
  2. Key vocabulary (12 min). Alphabet and numbers first: you say a letter, the student says the word, then swap. Then the procedure words. Read the example message aloud as a two-person dialogue.
  3. Exercises 1 and 2 (8 min). Sorting, then matching. Stop on roger and wilco, and on over and out: these are the pairs students mix up.
  4. Exercises 3 and 4 (8 min). Do Exercise 3 aloud first: you spell, the student writes, then they type. Exercise 4 can be homework if time is short.
  5. Reading (9 min). “A Clear Message.” Ask the student to find every procedure word in the text first, then answer the questions.
  6. Speaking (12 min). Question 1 as a quick drill. Then a short radio role-play on camera: you are Hotel One, the student is Bravo Two. Use a real place name they must spell and a grid or time they must read back.
  7. Prepositions and articles (6 min). Part A together; Parts B and C and the homework at home.

Key vocabulary: notes

Pronunciation

  • niner /ˈnaɪnə/: two syllables. It keeps 9 apart from German nein (no).
  • tree /triː/, fife /faɪf/, fower /ˈfaʊə/, ait /eɪt/: the radio forms. They avoid the th in three and keep 5 apart from fire.
  • roger /ˈrɒdʒə/ and wilco /ˈwɪlkəʊ/: stress on the first syllable.
  • Quebec /keˈbek/ (keh-BECK) and Juliett /ˌdʒuːliˈet/: the official NATO stress is on the last syllable.
  • Alfa /ˈælfə/ and Juliett are spelled this way on purpose, so that speakers of other languages say them correctly.
  • Charlie /ˈtʃɑːli/: French speakers often say sharlie; Spanish speakers sometimes say sarlie.

False friends and common mistakes

  • repeat: Spanish repetir, Italian ripetere, French répéter and Polish powtórz all lead to “Repeat, please.” On the radio, say say again. In some NATO procedures repeat has a different, fire-related meaning, so it is avoided.
  • over and out: Spanish cambio y corto, Italian passo e chiudo and Polish odbiór, bez odbioru make this feel natural, but in English procedure it is a contradiction. Over = reply, please; out = finished. Use one, not both. (French à vous = over, terminé = out.)
  • roger wilco: wilco already includes roger. Say Wilco, out.
  • roger as agreement: roger only means received. It does not mean yes or I will do it. This matters when an order is given.
  • Alpha and Juliet: the everyday English spellings. Accept them in speaking, but show the official Alfa and Juliett.
  • speak more slow: speak more slowly (adverb). Very common in all four language groups.
  • in the radio (Spanish en la radio, Italian alla radio): say on the radio.
  • Numbers: Polish and Italian speakers often group digits as in their language (trecento cinquantanove). On the radio, say each digit: tree fife niner.

Notes on the exercises

  • Exercise 1 (sort, 12 chips): the groups are about function, not grammar. If the student argues that copy could also be an answer to a radio check, agree: that is good thinking.
  • Exercise 2 (match, 6 pairs): ask the student to use each word in a two-line exchange after matching it.
  • Exercise 3 (type, 8 items): read them aloud first at natural speed. Then reverse it: the student spells a city for you.
  • Exercise 4 (word order, 6 sentences): all are high-frequency radio phrases. Drill them aloud once they are built.
  • Exercise 5 (reading): the key point is wrong (your read back was wrong) and correction (I made a mistake). The writing task is checked by Claude.
  • Exercise 7: Part A is on the radio, in contact with, by radio; Part B is articles with jobs and general nouns; Part C mixes both.

Extension for Level 3

  • Speaking question 4: push for a balanced argument with concession language: While it is true that ..., standard procedure still ...
  • Add the readability scale (loud and clear, weak but readable, strong but distorted, unreadable) and phrases like say again all after ..., say again all before ..., I spell, figures.
  • Give a longer message with two breaks and ask the student to read back only the key facts (who, where, when).
  • Discuss communication failures in well-known incidents (aviation is a good, non-sensitive source): what went wrong in the language, and what procedure would have helped?

Answers

  • Exercise 1: I received it: roger, wilco, copy, loud and clear. Repeat or check: say again, I say again, read back, radio check. Pause or end: over, out, wait one, break.
  • Exercise 2: roger = I have received your message.; wilco = I have received it, I understand and I will do it.; over = I have finished; please reply.; out = I have finished; no reply is needed.; correction = I made a mistake; here is the right version.; wait one = Please wait a few seconds..
  • Exercise 3: 1 Toronto, 2 Madrid, 3 Brussels, 4 Warsaw, 5 Naples, 6 Quebec, 7 349, 8 1058.
  • Exercise 4: 1 How do you read me? 2 I read you loud and clear. 3 Say again your last message. 4 Read back the time and the place. 5 Wait one while I check the map. 6 Spell your surname using the phonetic alphabet.
  • Exercise 5: 1 noise, 2 fife (five), 3 say, 4 seconds, 5 short, slowly (also: spell names with the phonetic alphabet, never be afraid to say say again); 6 open answer.
  • Exercise 7: Part A: 1 on, 2 to, 3 in, 4 by, 5 over, 6 on, 7 with, 8 on. Part B: 1 a, 2 The, 3 – (no article), 4 a, 5 an, 6 the, 7 a, 8 – (no article). Part C: 1 Can you say again your last message? 2 I didn't understand. Can you speak more slowly? 3 We are in contact with the headquarters. 4 She talks on the radio all day.
  • Exercise 6 and homework: open answers. Go through them together in the next lesson.
