What this lesson is for

Why this matters: tourists, drivers and colleagues from other countries need you to be clear, polite and firm, often when they are tired or stressed. You also need to report an incident in English so that others can act on it.

You will know

  • law-enforcement words: checkpoint, ID check, suspect, witness, victim, statement, evidence, fine
  • polite and firm instructions: May I see your driving licence, please? Please step out of the vehicle.

You will understand

  • when to be polite, when to be firm, and when to give a reason
  • which words are uncountable (evidence) and why we say the police are

You will be able to

  • give clear instructions to the public at a checkpoint or a border
  • report an incident: when, where, who, what happened and what was done

Common mistakes to watch for

  • He was fined of speeding. He was fined for speeding. Also arrested for, stopped for + the reason.
  • She is in duty tonight. She is on duty tonight. Also on patrol, but in custody.
  • Please go across passport control. Please go through passport control. At the checkpoint, at the border.
  • a witness of the accident; take a statement of the victim a witness to the accident; take a statement from the victim
  • Call emergency services. Call the emergency services. The police arrived. (the with services and known things: the details of the vehicle)
  • an evidence; the speeding causes accidents evidence; speeding causes accidents (No article for uncountable nouns and general ideas: smuggling.)
  • She is officer. There was accident. She is an officer. There was an accident. I need to make a statement. (a / an the first time, and with jobs)

Key vocabulary

Word or phraseMeaningExample
patrolofficers who move around an area to keep it safe; also the activityTwo officers are on patrol in the old town.
checkpoint / roadblocka place where vehicles and people are stopped and checked / a barrier that stops traffic on a roadThere is a roadblock after the accident.
ID checkasking a person to show an identity documentIt's a routine ID check.
suspecta person the police think may have committed a crimeThe suspect was seen near the station.
witnessa person who saw what happenedWe need to speak to every witness.
victima person who was hurt, or who suffered because of a crime or an accidentThe victim was taken to hospital.
incidentsomething that happens and needs police action or a reportThere was an incident at the petrol station.
to arrest / to detainto take a person to the police station because of a crime / to keep a person for a short time, for example for questionsHe was arrested for theft.
cautiona formal warning; in the UK, also the words an officer says to a person when arresting themHe got a caution, not a fine.
statementa written or spoken account of what a person saw or didThe witness signed her statement.
reportan official written description of an incidentI wrote the report at the end of my shift.
evidencefacts or objects that show what happened (uncountable: some evidence, a piece of evidence)The camera images are important evidence.
fine / ticketmoney you must pay for breaking a rule / the paper that tells you to pay itShe got a ticket for parking on the pavement.
speedingdriving faster than the speed limitHe was stopped for speeding.
breathalyser testa test that measures the alcohol in a driver's breath (also: breath test)All drivers at the checkpoint took a breathalyser test.
border control / passport controlthe place where officers check people and documents when they enter or leave a countryWe waited forty minutes at passport control.
smugglingbringing goods or people into a country illegallyTobacco smuggling is a problem near the border.
traffic accidenta crash involving vehicles (formal: collision)Nobody was hurt in the traffic accident.
emergency servicespolice, ambulance and fire services (112 in Europe, 911 in Canada)Call the emergency services.

Polite but firm: giving instructions to the public

TypeExamples
Polite questionGood evening. May I see your driving licence, please? Could you open the boot, please? (boot = trunk in Canada)
Polite instructionPlease turn off the engine. Please wait here.
Firm instructionI need you to stay in the vehicle. Please step out of the vehicle.
UrgentStop! Stay where you are. Step back, now.
Give a reasonThis is a routine check. It's for your safety. The road is closed because of an accident.

Say it right: SUSpect (noun) but susPECT (verb), WITness, EVidence, INcident, BREATHalyser (breth), SMUGgling.

Exercise 5: Reading

Summer at the Port

Every summer, hundreds of thousands of people travel through the port of Algeciras, in the south of Spain, on their way to North Africa. For Guardia Civil officer Lucía Herrera, it is the busiest time of the year. Her unit works in the port, where officers check vehicles and goods for smuggling, and on the roads nearby, where they deal with traffic.

Most of her work is helping people. “Many drivers are tired and stressed after a long journey,” she says. “A calm voice and a simple ‘Good evening, may I see your documents, please?’ make everything easier.” She often uses English with travellers from other countries, even when English is not their first language either.

Last July, during a routine check, her team found 3,000 packets of cigarettes hidden under the floor of a van. The driver was arrested for smuggling, and the cigarettes were kept as evidence. An hour later, there was a minor traffic accident in the queue: a car hit the back of a lorry. Nobody was hurt, but Lucía took statements from both drivers and a witness, and she wrote the report before the end of her shift.

Exercise 6: Speaking

Make a few notes for each question. We will talk about them in our lesson.

  1. Describe your job or your unit. What are your main duties on a normal shift?
  2. Role-play: you stop a car at a checkpoint. Greet the driver, ask for documents, explain why you stopped them, and end politely. (I will be the driver.)
  3. Describe a traffic accident or an incident you saw or dealt with: when, where, what happened and what was done. Use no names or sensitive details.
  4. Challenge: Some people think police officers should always explain the reason for an instruction; others say that in an emergency there is no time. When should an officer explain, and when should they simply give a firm instruction? Give examples.

Homework

Say when and where it happened, who was involved (driver, witness, victim), what happened, what you did and what the result was. Include at least two polite instructions you gave (for example Please wait here) and at least three passive verbs (was stopped, was taken, were kept).
