What this lesson is for

Why this matters: grid references, routes and supplies are passed by radio and in briefings every day, and one wrong number can send a convoy to the wrong place. You need to say them clearly and understand them the first time.

You will know

  • how to read and say a grid reference: right, then up
  • terrain words: ridge, valley, saddle, spur, ford, treeline, built-up area
  • logistics words: supplies, rations, fuel, spare parts, convoy, resupply

You will understand

  • which supply words are uncountable: fuel, equipment, ammunition (no -s)
  • how prepositions show a route: along, through, over, across

You will be able to

  • describe terrain and a route with directions and distances
  • talk about supplies and transport for a unit

Common mistakes to watch for

  • reading the northing first Read right, then up: the easting first, then the northing.
  • five kilometres of the camp five kilometres from the camp; ten kilometres north of the city
  • in the crossroads, in the left at the crossroads, at the ford (a point); on the left, on the right (a side)
  • We walked across the forest. We walked through the forest. Along a road or river, over a ridge or a bridge, across a river or a field.
  • We are short in fuel. We are short of fuel. (= not enough)
  • Moving supplies in Alps is difficult. Moving supplies in the Alps is difficult. Also the main road, the river Po. (the with known things, mountain ranges and rivers)
  • We need a fuel and an equipment. We need fuel and equipment. (No article for uncountable nouns and general plurals: rations, contour lines.)
  • There is ford near the farm. There is a ford near the farm. Also an alternative route, a spare part. (a / an the first time you mention something)

Warm-up

  • Is the area where you live or work flat or hilly? What are the main features?
  • Do you prefer a paper map or GPS? Why?
  • What supplies does your unit or workplace need every week?

Key vocabulary

Maps and directions

Word or phraseMeaningExample
grid referencenumbers that give an exact position on a mapThe supply point is at grid 482 615.
easting / northingthe first half of a grid reference (lines you read from left to right) / the second half (lines you read going up)The easting is 482 and the northing is 615.
read right, then upthe rule for grid references: “in the door, then up the stairs”Always read right, then up.
bearinga direction in degrees from north, measured clockwiseThe village is on a bearing of 090 degrees: due east.
contour linea line on a map that joins points of the same heightWhen contour lines are close together, the ground is steep.
routethe way you plan to travel from one place to anotherWe took the northern route because of the snow.
checkpoint (on a route)a fixed point on a route where you check your position or reportCall me when you reach Checkpoint 3.

Grid references: read right, then up. In grid 482 615, go right along the bottom to 482 (the easting: in the door), then up to 615 (the northing: up the stairs). On the radio: grid fower ait too, six wun fife.

Compass: north, north-east, east, south-east, south, south-west, west, north-west. Due north = exactly north. A bearing has three figures: zero niner zero degrees (090) = due east.

Distances: 500 metres, about two kilometres (informal: two klicks), five kilometres north of the town.

Terrain

Word or phraseMeaningExample
ridgea long, narrow line of high groundThe road runs along the top of the ridge.
valleylow land between hills or mountains, often with a riverThe village is at the bottom of the valley.
saddlea low point between two hilltopsThe track crosses the saddle between the two hills.
spurhigh ground that goes out from a hill or ridge, like a fingerThe observation post is on a spur above the river.
forda shallow place where you can cross a river on foot or by vehicleThe trucks crossed the river at the ford.
treelinethe edge of a wood or forestThe vehicles stopped along the treeline.
built-up areaa town or village with many buildings close togetherDrive slowly in built-up areas.

Logistics

Word or phraseMeaningExample
suppliesthe things a unit needs: food, water, fuel, equipmentThe supplies arrive every Tuesday.
rationsfood packs for soldiers in the fieldWe have rations for three days.
fuelpetrol or diesel for vehicles and generators (uncountable)The trucks need more fuel.
ammunitiona general category of military supplies (uncountable: no -s)Ammunition is stored separately from fuel.
spare partsnew parts to replace broken onesWe are waiting for spare parts for the generator.
convoya group of vehicles that travel togetherThe convoy left at dawn with twelve trucks.
resupplybringing new supplies to a unit (noun and verb)The next resupply is on Friday.
supply pointa place where units collect suppliesThe supply point is next to the airfield.

Say it right: route (root; also rowt in Canada), teRRAIN, CONtour, VALley, ford (like Ford the car), RAtions (RASH-uns), KILometres or kiLOMetres.

Exercise 5: Reading

Moving the Supplies

Sergeant Major Paola Ferri is in charge of logistics for an Italian Alpine battalion. This week her job is to plan the resupply of a company that is training in the mountains, forty kilometres north of the base. The convoy has six trucks: two carry fuel, two carry rations and water, and two carry spare parts and other equipment.

The planned route follows the valley road to a ford, crosses the river and climbs to a supply point at grid 482 615, below a long ridge. But after two days of heavy rain, the ford is too deep for the trucks. Paola studies the map. A second route goes over a saddle between two hills. It is six kilometres longer, and the contour lines show a steep climb, but the road is paved all the way.

She chooses the longer route and sends the new checkpoints to every driver. “In logistics, the shortest route is not always the fastest,” she says. “A truck stuck in a river delivers nothing.”
