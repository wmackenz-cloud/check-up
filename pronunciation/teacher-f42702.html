Reading the phonetic alphabet

The International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) gives every sound one symbol, so you can see a sound that English spelling hides. Though, through, tough and cough all use ough, but in IPA they are /ðəʊ/, /θruː/, /tʌf/ and /kɒf/. Dictionaries such as Cambridge and Oxford Learner’s use it, and many Asian students learnt it at school, so it gives you a shared way to point at a sound.

  • Slashes /ʃ/ mean a sound as the language uses it (a phoneme): a difference that changes meaning. Square brackets [tʰ] show how it is actually said, with details such as aspiration.
  • ˈ marks the stressed syllable and goes before it: /ˈbɪznəs/ = BUSI-ness. ː means a long vowel: /iː/ in sheep, /ɪ/ in ship.
  • Voiced and voiceless pairs use the same mouth position. Only the voice box changes: /p b/, /t d/, /k g/, /f v/, /θ ð/, /s z/, /ʃ ʒ/, /tʃ dʒ/. Hand on the throat: it buzzes for the voiced one.
  • British or North American? Most symbols are the same. The main differences are /əʊ/ (UK) and /oʊ/ (US) in go; /ɒ/ (UK) and /ɑː/ (US) in hot; and the r after vowels, which is pronounced in North America (/kɑːr/) but not in the UK (/kɑː/). Teach the accent you speak and accept both.

Spelling traps from the home language

Both groups learnt to read their own language in Roman letters (Vietnamese quốc ngữ and Chinese pinyin), so they read English letters with home values. It helps to name the trap.

  • Vietnamese: th = aspirated t; d and gi = /z/ (North) or /j/ (South); r = /z/ (North); x = /s/; ph = /f/; đ is the d sound.
  • Pinyin: b, d, g are unaspirated p, t, k (not voiced); z = ts; c = tsʰ; x = a soft sh; q = a soft ch; r = a buzzy retroflex sound; e = a sound close to /ə/.

The same, and different

  • Shared problems: word endings and clusters, th, short/long vowels, and rhythm. Both are tonal, syllable-timed languages, so both groups give every syllable the same length and use pitch for tone, not for stress.
  • Mainly Chinese: /v/ and /w/, voiced endings (/b d g/ as /p t k/), /l/ and /r/, /n/ and /ŋ/ (southern), and adding vowels after final consonants.
  • Mainly Vietnamese: dropped (not padded) endings, initial /p/, /r/ as /z/ and /s/ with /ʃ/ (Northern), and /dʒ/.
  • Cantonese speakers (Hong Kong, Guangdong, many Chinese Canadians) are closer to Vietnamese: their final /p t k/ are unreleased, and /n/ and /l/ often merge.
  • Where to start: Vietnamese: endings, then th. Chinese: endings and voicing together, then /v/ and /w/, then th and the short/long vowels.

Drill notes: a 10-minute routine

  1. Warm up (1 minute): the position or lip shapes from Drill A of the sound you are working on.
  2. Model and repeat (3 minutes): you say it, they repeat. Exaggerate first.
  3. Swap roles (3 minutes): they say one word from a pair, you guess which. This tests whether the sound really changed.
  4. Sentences (2 minutes): the last part of the drill, at normal speed.
  5. Homework (1 minute): send the drill link. They record it on the page and you get an email.

Start with the area that hurt their Reading Check most. For most Vietnamese speakers that is endings, then th. For most Chinese speakers it is endings and voicing, then /v/ and /w/. Do Joining Words alongside endings: it makes endings easier.

Drill 1: Word Endings

Goes with Lesson 2. Open the student page

Vietnamese syllables end in only a few consonants, and they are not released: the mouth closes but no air comes out. There are no consonant clusters at the end of a syllable. So worked often comes out as work, and friends as fren.

  • Listen for: dropped -ed and -s, final stops with no release, and an added vowel (work-uh).
  • Drill A: you say one side (now or yesterday), the student says which. Then swap.
  • Drill B: the back-chaining ladders are the fix for clusters. Keep the ending loud and slow; speed comes later.
  • Drill D: the answers are in the page. Ask for a full sentence each time, not just the verb.
  • Chinese speakers: they often add a vowel instead of dropping the ending (work-uh, bed-uh), and turn voiced endings into voiceless ones (bag as back). Ask them to stop the sound and hold the mouth closed: no extra vowel. For voicing, lengthen the vowel before a voiced ending: bag has a longer a than back.
  • Quick cue: "Finish the word" plus a hand gesture closing at the end of each word.

Drill 2: The th Sound

Goes with Lesson 3. Open the student page

Vietnamese has no th. Students usually replace it with t or d, sometimes s or z. The tongue position is the whole problem, so make it visible.

  • Listen for: three as tree, think as tink, they as day or zay, with as wit.
  • Drill A: ask them to turn on the camera and check their tongue tip is visible. Exaggerate first; it relaxes naturally later.
  • Drill B: say a word from a row and have them point to the column, then swap roles.
  • Drill D: numbers and dates are high value for exams; drill them at speed once the sound is stable.
  • Chinese speakers: listen for sink for think, free for three, zis for this. The fix is the same: tongue tip between the teeth, then blow. If they also have /v/ problems, do Drill A with the lip shape for /v/ (top teeth on the bottom lip) next to the th shape, so they feel the difference.
  • Accept a slightly exaggerated th. It is better than t.

Drill 3: s, sh and z

Goes with Lesson 3. Open the student page

Depending on the region, s and sh may sound the same to a Vietnamese speaker, and final s and z are often dropped. Lips are the easiest thing to correct, because they are visible.

  • Listen for: see and she merged, final s missing, price and prize the same.
  • Drill A: model the lip shapes on camera. Flat smile for s, rounded and forward for sh.
  • Drill C: hand on the throat for z. Many students have never noticed voicing.
  • Drill D: read once slowly with the lip shapes exaggerated, then at normal speed.
  • Chinese speakers: /s/ and /ʃ/ are usually fine. The problem is /z/, which comes out as /s/ or dz (pinyin z). Use Drill C: hand on the throat, and hold a long zzzz like a bee before the word. Add /v/ and /w/ here too: vest, west, very, worry, with the teeth on the lip for /v/ and round lips for /w/.

Drill 4: Joining Words

Goes with Lesson 5. Open the student page

Linking is the hidden fix for endings: an unreleased final consonant becomes the start of the next syllable, where it is easy to say. Many students speak word by word, which sounds choppy.

  • Listen for: a pause or glottal stop between every word.
  • Drill B is the key one for Vietnamese speakers: worked in as work-tin brings back the -ed.
  • Drill C: the tiny w and y glides stop students inserting a stop between vowels.
  • Ask them to read a sentence from their own speaking with the joins marked.
  • Chinese speakers: linking also stops the extra vowel. bed-uh and becomes be-dand. Point out that the final consonant moves to the next word, so there is no gap for a vowel to fill.

Drill 5: Rhythm and Stress

Goes with Lesson 4. Open the student page

Vietnamese is syllable-timed and every syllable carries a tone, so students often give each syllable equal length and weight. English is stress-timed: strong syllables are long, small words shrink.

  • Listen for: every word the same length, full vowels in to, of, and, can, and stress on the wrong syllable in long words.
  • Drill A: tap along with them. The lines should take nearly the same time; that is the point.
  • Drill B: model the weak forms, then contrast with the full form so they hear the difference.
  • Drill C: have them clap the stressed syllable. Watch for last-syllable stress (busi-NESS).
  • Drill D: contrastive stress is easy to practise as a game; you say the wrong fact, they correct you.
  • Chinese speakers: Mandarin is also syllable-timed, and pitch carries tone, so a stressed syllable may come out higher but not longer or louder. Teach stress as longer and louder, not just higher. Weak forms (/tə/, /əv/, /ən/) are as new to them as to Vietnamese speakers.
