What this lesson is for

Why this matters: many English nouns and verbs always go with the same small word (a preposition), and it is often different from your language. If you choose the wrong one, people still understand you, but it sounds wrong straight away. This lesson fixes the ones you will use most.

You will know

  • 15 common nouns and verbs with their preposition: a rise in, the reason for, rely on, deal with
  • which verbs take no preposition at all: discuss, explain + the thing
  • the direct object (the thing) and the indirect object (the person who gets it)

You will understand

  • why you learn the word and its preposition together, as one block
  • why a preposition is followed by a noun or an -ing form
  • why you ask Verb + WHAT? to find the direct object, and never trust word order alone

You will be able to

  • talk about changes, reasons and problems at work and in the news
  • spot and fix the most common preposition mistakes
  • put the thing first and the person after to or for: explain the rules to me

Common mistakes to watch for

  • The most common one: putting the person straight after the verb. Can you explain me the rules? Can you explain the rules to me? Ask explain + WHAT? The rules. The thing comes first; the person comes after to.
  • She described me the problem. She described the problem to me.
  • He suggested me a good hotel. He suggested a good hotel to me. (Or: He suggested that I stay at a good hotel.)
  • Please send to me the report. Please send me the report, or send the report to me. Never to + person before the thing.
  • I bought a present to my mother. I bought a present for my mother. (for = something you do for someone)
  • It depends of the price. It depends on the price.
  • We discussed about the plan. We discussed the plan. (No preposition after discuss.)
  • She apologised me. She apologised to me.
  • an increase of prices an increase in prices (but an increase of 10%)
  • the difference of A and B the difference between A and B

Let's talk

Every day we talk about changes, reasons and problems. Prices go up. Something breaks, and someone has to fix it. We need help, so we ask a friend we trust. In English, these ideas often use a noun or a verb with its own small preposition: a rise in prices, the reason for the delay, rely on a friend, deal with a problem.

You hear these phrases at work, in the news and in everyday conversation. If you choose the wrong preposition, people usually still understand you, but it sounds wrong straight away. In this lesson you will learn 15 of the most useful ones.

Warm-up: talk about these questions with your teacher. Don't worry about getting the prepositions right yet. Just say what you think. We will come back to them at the end.

  • Who do you rely on when you have a problem? Who relies on you?
  • What's the biggest difference between life in your country and life here?
  • Has there been a rise in prices where you live? What has gone up the most? What is the reason for it, do you think?
  • What kind of problems do you deal with at work or at home?
  • Is there a big demand for your job (or the job you want) in this country?
  • When you learn English, what do you focus on: speaking, grammar or new words?
  • What did your parents not approve of when you were young?

Before you start

Before this: Prepositional Phrases 1: Fixed Phrases (on purpose, in advance, out of order). This lesson adds more nouns and verbs that choose their own preposition, like the ones in Words and Prepositions.

They are very common at work and in the news: a rise in prices, access to the files, rely on someone, deal with a problem.

Learn the word and its preposition together, as one block. Don't translate the preposition from your language: it is often different. depend on, not depend of.

And remember from Words and Prepositions: after a preposition, use a noun or the -ing form: I apologised for being late.

Noun + prepositionMeaningExample
a rise / an increase / a fall in (review)the thing that goes up or downThere's been a rise in rents.
a reason for (review)why something happensWhat's the reason for the delay?
the cause ofwhat made something happenThe cause of the fire is not known.
an effect ona change that something makesStress has an effect on your sleep.
damage toharm to a thingThe storm did a lot of damage to the roof.
a demand forpeople want or need itThere's a big demand for nurses.
a difference betweenhow two things are not the sameWhat's the difference between these two phones?
access tothe right or the way to use or enter somethingAll staff have access to the gym.

in or of? Use in for the thing that changes, and of for the amount: a rise in rents, a rise of 6%. Both together: a rise of 6% in rents.

Group 3: Who gets what? The thing and the person

Many verbs have two objects: a thing and a person.

The direct object is the thing. To find it, always ask Verb + WHAT? Explain + what? The rules. Send + what? The report.

The indirect object is the person who gets the thing. Ask to whom? or for whom? To me. For my mother.

The safe pattern: verb + the thing + to / for + the person.

Can you explain the rules to me? She described the problem to her manager. I bought a present for my mother.

Word order can move, so never trust position alone. With a long object, English often puts the short part first: Can you explain to me how it works? Ask explain + WHAT? The answer is still how it works, not me. The person always keeps its to.

Verb groupPerson straight after the verb?Examples
explain, describe, say, suggest, recommend, introduce, mention, report, announceNever. Always thing + to + person.explain the problem to me, say hello to her, introduce my wife to you, recommend a doctor to him
give, send, show, offer, lend, pay, bring, teach, writeYes, both are fine, with no to when the person comes first.send me the file = send the file to me; show them the photos = show the photos to them
buy, make, get, book, cook, findYes, both are fine, but the person takes for.buy her a coffee = buy a coffee for her; book us a table = book a table for us
tell, askPerson first.tell me the truth, ask him a question

With it and them: put the thing first. Send it to me, not Send me it. Give them to her.

to or for? to = the person receives it (give, send, explain, describe). for = you do something to help or please them (buy, make, book, cook).
