What this lesson is for

Why this matters: adjectives say what something is like. Adverbs say how something is done: the piano plays softly. They help you describe actions clearly, and the tricky ones cause very common mistakes.

You will know

  • how to make adverbs: soft → softly, gentle → gently, happy → happily
  • the irregular ones: good → well, and fast, hard, late that don't change
  • comment adverbs like Luckily, and Unfortunately,

You will understand

  • that an adjective describes a thing, and an adverb describes an action
  • why we use an adjective after be, look, sound, feel and seem

You will be able to

  • describe how music is played and how people do things
  • put adverbs in the right place in a sentence

Common mistakes to watch for

  • She sings very good. She sings very well.
  • He drives fastly. He drives fast.
  • He works very hardly. He works very hard. (hardly means almost not.)
  • The concert started lately. The concert started late. (lately means recently.)
  • The music sounds beautifully. The music sounds beautiful. (Adjective after sound.)
  • She plays quietly the piano. She plays the piano quietly. (Don't put the adverb between the verb and its object.)

The rule: adjective or adverb?

An adjective describes a thing or a person. An adverb describes an action (a verb).

She is a careful driver. → She drives carefully.

Watch out: after be, look, sound, feel, seem we use an adjective: The music sounds beautiful. sounds beautifully

Adverbs can also make adjectives stronger: incredibly loud, really quiet.

The rule: tricky adverbs

  • good → well: She's a good singer. She sings well.
  • fast → fast: a fast car, he drives fast fastly
  • hard → hard: He works hard. But hardly means "almost not": I can hardly hear it.
  • late → late: The train arrived late. But lately means "recently": I've been busy lately.

The rule: where does the adverb go?

How often (always, usually, often, sometimes, never): before the main verb, but after be. She always plays. He is never late.

How (quietly, well, carefully): usually at the end, after the object. She plays the piano quietly. She plays quietly the piano.

The rule: comment adverbs

Some adverbs come at the start of a sentence and show how you feel about the whole thing. Put a comma after them.

Unfortunately, it rained all day. Luckily, we had umbrellas.

Part 1: tap an adverb, then tap what it means.

  • Unfortunately, = This is bad news.
  • Luckily, = This is good news, and it happened by chance.
  • Surprisingly, = I didn't expect this.
  • Hopefully, = I hope this will happen.
  • Personally, = This is my own opinion.
  • Obviously, = Everyone can see or knows this.

Exercise 6: Speaking game: In the manner of the word

Play this with your teacher or a friend.

  1. One person secretly chooses an adverb: nervously, angrily, happily, slowly, sleepily, proudly, carefully, lazily.
  2. The others give them a task to act out: Eat your breakfast. Open a present. Answer the phone. Walk to the door.
  3. They act it out in the manner of the adverb. Everyone guesses: "Are you eating angrily?"
  4. Take turns. Then talk: how do you usually do these things?
