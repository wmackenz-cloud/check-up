What this lesson is for

Why this matters: nearly every description uses adjectives and adverbs, and English is strict about which is which. Asking one simple question helps you choose the right word every time.

You will know

  • the question to ask: what does the word describe, a thing or an action?
  • the words that trick you: friendly, lovely (adjectives), fast, hard, late (both), well

You will understand

  • why look, sound, feel, smell and taste often take an adjective
  • the difference between real and really, sure and surely

You will be able to

  • describe a photo with an adjective and an adverb
  • find and fix adjective and adverb mistakes

Common mistakes to watch for

  • He drives careful. He drives carefully. (It describes the action.)
  • She looks beautifully in that photo. She looks beautiful in that photo.
  • I feel badly about it. I feel bad about it.
  • The film was real good. The film was really good.
  • She smiled friendlily. She smiled in a friendly way. (friendly is an adjective. There is no adverb.)
  • They speak English fluent. They speak English fluently.

Before you start

This lesson puts it all together. Most sentences need an adjective and an adverb.

The quiet piano plays softly.   A man walks quickly across the wet street.

The rule: one question to ask

What does the word describe?

A thing or a person (a noun) → adjective: a careful driver, the wet street

An action (a verb) → adverb: she drives carefully, he walks quickly

Another adjective or adverb → adverb: incredibly cosy, terribly slowly

Watch out: after be, look, sound, feel, seem use an adjective: It looks beautiful. I feel bad.

The rule: words that trick you

  • -ly adjectives: friendly, lovely, lonely, silly, likely, costly, elderly are adjectives. There is no adverb: say in a friendly way. She smiled friendlily. an elderly man, a costly mistake, It's likely to rain.
  • Same word for both: fast, hard, late, early, daily, straight, high, far: a fast train / it goes fast; an early start / we left early; a daily newspaper / it comes out daily; a straight road / go straight on; a high wall / jump high; the far end of the room / don't go far.
  • Irregular: good → well. But well is also an adjective that means healthy: I'm well, thanks. I don't feel well.
  • real / really: real is the adjective, really is the adverb: a real problem; really difficult. The film was real good. The film was really good.
  • sure / surely: Are you sure? (adjective: certain) Surely you're joking! (adverb: I can't believe it)

The rule: linking verbs

look, sound, feel, smell, taste, seem, become are often linking verbs: like be, they link the subject to a word that describes it, so we use an adjective: It smells delicious. She looks happy. That sounds great. I feel bad. But when the verb is an action that someone does, use an adverb: She looks carefully at the map. (She is doing the looking.) She looks careful. (That's how she seems to us.) Test: if you can say is instead (She looks careful → She is careful), use an adjective.
