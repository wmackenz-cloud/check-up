Quick reminder

The as ... as similes compare one quality (as cold as ice). The like similes in this lesson compare a whole scene: a person, an animal or a situation that everyone can picture. When you say a new manager is like a bull in a china shop, the listener sees the bull, the fragile plates and the damage, all at once.

  • like a kid in a candy store: delighted and excited, especially by having lots of things to choose from
  • like a deer in the headlights: frozen with shock or fear, unable to think or act
  • like a bull in a china shop: clumsy or tactless in a situation that needs care
  • like talking to a brick wall: the other person doesn't listen or respond at all
  • like water off a duck's back: criticism or insults have no effect on someone
  • like a red rag to a bull: certain to make a particular person very angry
  • like a broken record: saying the same thing again and again, annoyingly
  • like a cat on a hot tin roof: nervous, restless and unable to keep still

Watch out for

  • He just stood there like a deer in the lights. Like a deer in the headlights. The full noun is fixed; British speakers also say like a rabbit in the headlights.
  • My manager repeats himself like a broken disc. Like a broken record. A literal translation (French disque rayé, Spanish disco rayado) doesn't work in English.
  • Criticism is like water on a duck's back to her. Like water off a duck's back. The water runs off; the preposition is fixed.
