What this lesson is for

You will be able to

  • describe tasks, objects, evidence and health with natural similes
  • use the casual form without the first as, and the about as ... as pattern
  • recognise the history behind dead as a doornail in Shakespeare and Dickens

You will know

  • eight as ... as similes for things
  • British: safe as houses, cheap as chips

You will understand

  • literal to abstract: cold as ice, dead as a doornail
  • ironic similes: as clear as mud

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Her hands were as cold as snow. As cold as ice. Snow is understandable but it isn't the fixed simile.
  • Don't worry, the recipe is as easy as cake. As easy as pie, or a piece of cake. Don't mix the two.
  • After a week in bed I feel right like rain. I feel as right as rain (or right as rain). Never like in this simile.
  • Your money is safe like houses in a Canadian bank. Safe as houses, and it's British. In Canada, perfectly safe or as safe as can be is more natural.
  • The proof is clear as the day. Clear as day, with no article. You can also say as plain as day.

The big idea

With things and situations, as ... as similes mostly work as strong intensifiers. As easy as pie simply means very easy; as old as the hills means extremely old. The image is memorable, but nobody thinks about pies or hills.

Some of these similes have travelled from the physical to the abstract. A doornail is literally a lump of iron, but a project, a law or a marriage can be as dead as a doornail. Cold as ice describes your hands, but also an unfriendly person.

Irony is common: as clear as mud means confusing, and about as useful as a chocolate teapot (British) means useless. Listen for the tone of voice: the same frame can praise or mock.

You already use it:

  • Thanks for the instructions. They're as clear as mud.
  • The lake was like glass at six in the morning.
  • Turn on the fan; this room is like an oven.
  • The highway across the prairies is as straight as an arrow.
  • The house was as quiet as a tomb after the guests left.
  • Leave the bread out overnight and it'll be as hard as a rock.
  • The new software runs like a dream.

Meaning: extremely light in weight neutral

Bend it: Often light as a feather. Also feather-light and light as air (for food: the soufflé was light as air).

Note: Can describe a person being carried, or a feeling of relief. Not as light as a plume.

iceas cold as iceThe cottage was as cold as ice when we arrived in January.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: very cold; also emotionally cold and unfriendly neutral

Bend it: Ice-cold as an adjective (an ice-cold drink). For people: she was cold as ice to him.

Note: Common wrong version: as cold as snow. For drinks, ice-cold is positive; for people, cold as ice is negative.

the hillsas old as the hillsThat excuse is as old as the hills; teachers have heard it for decades.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: extremely old; very well known for a long time casual

Bend it: Usually about ideas, jokes, tricks or problems. Also as old as time, which is more poetic.

Note: Saying a person is as old as the hills is teasing and can offend. For people, use it only about yourself.

day (bright daylight)as clear as dayFrom the security video it was as clear as day who had taken the parcel.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: completely obvious or easy to see neutral

Bend it: Also as plain as day. No article before day. Crystal clear is more about being easy to understand.

Note: Opposite, ironic: as clear as mud. Don't say clear as the day.

a pieas easy as pieOnce you know the shortcut, the tax form is as easy as pie.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: very easy casual

Bend it: Easy as pie is most common. Near-synonyms: a piece of cake, easy as ABC, easy peasy (childish or playful).

Note: Mainly North American but understood everywhere. Don't mix it into as easy as cake.

a doornail (a big nail hammered into an old door)as dead as a doornailAfter the vote, the pipeline proposal was as dead as a doornail.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: completely dead; also completely finished or failed humorous

Bend it: Used for plans, deals, batteries and phones as well as people and animals. Often dead as a doornail.

Note: Recorded since the 1300s. For something extinct or out of date, as dead as a dodo is better.

Meaning: perfectly healthy or fine again, especially after illness casual

Bend it: Almost always be/feel/look right as rain, often in the future: you'll be right as rain. Fixed: no like.

Note: Originally British, common in Canada, slightly old-fashioned and reassuring. Said by doctors, parents and grandparents.

Meaning: very safe or secure, with no risk casual

Bend it: British; rare in North America. Fixed plural houses. Often ironic since housing crashes.

Note: Canadians and Americans would say perfectly safe, a safe bet or rock-solid.

In literature, poetry and music

Writers and songwriters use the same big idea. Read about each one, then find it and read or listen together in the lesson.

Literature

A Christmas Carol

Charles Dickens, 1843

Dickens opens the story by insisting that Scrooge's partner Marley is as dead as a doornail. The narrator then jokes that he can't see why a doornail is especially dead, and would prefer a coffin nail, but won't argue with his ancestors' wisdom.

Why do you think Dickens starts a ghost story by insisting so strongly that someone is dead?

Find it
Literature

Henry VI, Part 2

William Shakespeare, c. 1591

The rebel leader Jack Cade, hungry and hiding in a garden, threatens to leave its owner as dead as a doornail. Shakespeare didn't invent the simile, which was already centuries old, but he shows how common it was in everyday speech.

Which similes in your language are hundreds of years old? Do people still use them?

Find it
Poetry

The Rime of the Ancient Mariner

Samuel Taylor Coleridge, 1798

When the wind dies, the mariner's ship is stuck for days on a flat, motionless ocean. Coleridge compares it to a painted ship on a painted sea: as still and lifeless as a picture. The as ... as frame makes the stillness feel unbearable.

Describe a moment in your life when everything seemed to stop, using an <em>as ... as</em> simile.

Find it
Music

Cold as Ice

Foreigner, 1977

The title simile is the whole message: the singer accuses someone of being cold as ice, emotionally hard and willing to hurt others to get what she wants, and warns that she'll regret it. It shows the simile moving from temperature to personality.

Is calling someone cold as ice worse than calling them unfriendly? What does the image add?
