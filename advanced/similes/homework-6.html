Quick reminder

A simile compares two unlike things openly, with a signal word: like, as, as if, the way, than. A metaphor drops the signal and says one thing is another. A simile is a little more modest and more precise: it says in this one way, these two things are alike.

  • as + adjective + as: compares one quality to something that has a lot of it
  • like + noun: compares an action or a thing to a picture
  • as if / as though + clause: compares with an imagined situation
  • the way + clause: compares how two actions are done
  • more ... than: compares by saying something goes beyond a familiar extreme
  • extended simile: a simile developed over several clauses or sentences
  • epic (Homeric) simile: a long, detailed comparison that becomes a small scene of its own
  • turning a cliché: refreshing an old simile by twisting, extending or contradicting it

Watch out for

  • The office was as quiet like a library. As quiet as a library. The as ... as frame needs the second as.
  • She sang as a bird at the wedding. She sang like a bird. As a bird means in the role of a bird.
  • He looked at me like I was a ghost, in his report to the board. In formal writing, prefer as if I were a ghost. Like + clause is fine in casual speech.

Exercise 1: Match the expression to its meaning

Tap an expression, then tap its meaning.

  • simile = an open comparison using like, as, than or the way
  • metaphor = says one thing is another
  • cliché = a comparison so familiar it's predictable
  • fresh simile = a specific, surprising but clear comparison
  • mixed metaphor = two images that clash
  • as dead as a doornail = completely dead (cliché played with by Dickens)
