What this lesson is for

Why this matters: Native speakers often describe a feeling or a situation by pointing to a vivid little scene: a deer frozen in the headlights, a bull loose among the teacups. These similes let you say a lot about mood and behaviour in very few words.

You will know

  • the frame like + noun phrase after verbs such as feel, look, sound, act and it's
  • eight common similes for feelings, reactions and frustrating situations

You will understand

  • why like (not as) introduces these comparisons
  • which similes are British, which are North American, and which are shared
  • how a like simile differs from a metaphor that makes the same point

You will be able to

  • describe your own reactions vividly: delight, panic, relief, restlessness
  • complain diplomatically about someone who won't listen or keeps repeating themselves
  • recognise when a simile has been shortened into a metaphor or a compound adjective

Common mistakes to watch for

  • He just stood there like a deer in the lights. Like a deer in the headlights. The full noun is fixed; British speakers also say like a rabbit in the headlights.
  • My manager repeats himself like a broken disc. Like a broken record. A literal translation (French disque rayé, Spanish disco rayado) doesn't work in English.
  • Criticism is like water on a duck's back to her. Like water off a duck's back. The water runs off; the preposition is fixed.
  • He walked into the meeting as a bull in a china shop. Like a bull in a china shop. As a bull would mean he really is a bull, in that role.
  • I was like a kid in a candy store, like a cat on a hot tin roof and like a deer in the headlights. Pick one simile. Stacking clichés sounds comic or careless, not vivid.

The big idea

The as ... as similes compare one quality (as cold as ice). The like similes in this lesson compare a whole scene: a person, an animal or a situation that everyone can picture. When you say a new manager is like a bull in a china shop, the listener sees the bull, the fragile plates and the damage, all at once.

These similes usually follow verbs of perception and behaviour: feel like, look like, sound like, act like, or the frame it's like + a situation (it's like talking to a brick wall). They are mostly casual or humorous, so they suit conversation, emails to colleagues you know, and storytelling more than formal reports.

Many of them can lose the word like and become metaphors: she's a bull in a china shop, that's a red rag to a bull, a deer-in-the-headlights look. Keeping like sounds slightly softer; dropping it sounds more confident and more judgmental. One trap: the casual quotative she was like, "No way!" is not a simile at all.

You already use it:

  • The whole wedding went like clockwork.
  • After the hike I slept like a log.
  • The news spread like wildfire on social media.
  • Her new novel is selling like hot cakes.
  • A bit of vinegar on the stain worked like a charm.
  • The jacket fits like a glove.
  • My teenage son eats like a horse.

like a kid in a candy storeGive my dad an afternoon in a hardware store and he's like a kid in a candy store.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: delighted and excited, especially by having lots of things to choose from humorous

Bend it: Usually after be or feel: I felt like a kid in a candy store. British version: like a child in a sweet shop.

Note: Region: candy store is North American, sweet shop British. Almost always used about adults, which is why it's funny. Can also hint at a lack of self-control.

like a deer in the headlightsWhen the reporter asked about the missing funds, the spokesperson looked like a deer in the headlights.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: frozen with shock or fear, unable to think or act casual

Bend it: Also like a deer caught in the headlights, and as a compound adjective: a deer-in-the-headlights look.

Note: Mainly North American. British speakers more often say like a rabbit in the headlights. Not the same as nervous: the key idea is freezing.

like a bull in a china shopHe handled the merger talks like a bull in a china shop and offended both families.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: clumsy or tactless in a situation that needs care casual

Bend it: Often after act, behave, go in; also as a metaphor: he's a bull in a china shop.

Note: China here means porcelain dishes, so lower case. It can describe physical clumsiness or, more often at C1 level, social and diplomatic clumsiness.

like talking to a brick wallI've asked the landlord about the heating five times. It's like talking to a brick wall.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: the other person doesn't listen or respond at all casual

Bend it: Usually it's like talking to a brick wall; also I might as well be talking to a brick wall.

Note: Don't confuse with like banging your head against a brick wall, which means making a frustrating effort that achieves nothing.

like water off a duck's backThe coach yells a lot, but it's like water off a duck's back to the veterans.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: criticism or insults have no effect on someone casual

Bend it: Often it's (just) like water off a duck's back (to him). Sometimes shortened: water off a duck's back.

Note: Can be admiring (she's resilient) or critical (he never learns). Ducks' feathers are oily, so water runs off: the preposition is off.

like a red rag to a bullMentioning the parking fines at the council meeting was like a red rag to a bull.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: certain to make a particular person very angry casual

Bend it: Often without like: it's a red rag to a bull. North Americans tend to say like waving a red flag in front of a bull.

Note: Mainly British. Fun fact: bulls don't react to the colour red; it's the movement of the cape. The idiom doesn't care.

like a broken recordI know I sound like a broken record, but please save your work before you leave.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: saying the same thing again and again, annoyingly casual

Bend it: Usually sound like a broken record. British speakers also say like a stuck record.

Note: From vinyl records that skip and repeat. Very common as a polite self-aware apology before repeating a reminder.

like a cat on a hot tin roofWaiting for the biopsy results, she was like a cat on a hot tin roof all week.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: nervous, restless and unable to keep still neutral

Bend it: Usually after be; British speakers also say like a cat on hot bricks, the older form.

Note: Made famous by Tennessee Williams's 1955 play title. Not the same as a deer in the headlights: the cat can't stop moving; the deer can't move.

In literature, poetry and music

Writers and songwriters use the same big idea. Read about each one, then find it and read or listen together in the lesson.

Literature

Cat on a Hot Tin Roof

Tennessee Williams, 1955

In this Pulitzer Prize-winning play set on a Mississippi plantation, Maggie describes her own state with the title image: anxious, unwanted by her husband Brick, but refusing to jump off. The simile captures a feeling of nervous endurance rather than panic.

When have you felt like a cat on a hot tin roof but decided to stay where you were?

Literature

Wuthering Heights

Emily Brontë, 1847

Catherine explains her two loves to the housekeeper Nelly with two similes. Her love for the gentle Edgar Linton is like the leaves in a wood, which time will change; her love for Heathcliff is like the permanent rock underneath, not pleasant, but necessary. The contrast says more than any adjective could.

Catherine compares love to leaves and to rock. What would you compare a lasting friendship to?

Find it
Literature

Romeo and Juliet

William Shakespeare, c. 1595

In the balcony scene, Romeo calls Juliet the sun: that's a metaphor, she is the sun. Juliet, more cautious, uses a simile: she says her love is as deep and limitless as the sea, and the more she gives, the more she has. Compare how bold the metaphor feels next to the simile.

Which sounds more convincing to you, the metaphor or the simile? Why?

Music

Bird on the Wire

Leonard Cohen, 1969

The Montreal-born songwriter wrote it on the Greek island of Hydra. He compares himself to a bird perched on a wire and to other unsettled figures, and confesses that he has tried, in his own way, to be free. The song works as both an apology and a self-portrait.

What image would you choose to describe yourself at a restless time in your life?
