What this lesson is for

You will be able to

  • describe people's personality, health and mood vividly but naturally
  • drop the first as in casual speech and recognise playful variations
  • avoid stacking cliches and choose one simile that really fits

You will know

  • as + adjective + as + noun
  • eight similes for character, health and mood

You will understand

  • fixed comparison nouns (no swapping)
  • North American, British or old-fashioned similes

Common mistakes to watch for

  • She stayed as cool like a cucumber during the inspection. She stayed as cool as a cucumber. The frame is always as ... as, never as ... like.
  • My grandfather is as fit as a violin at ninety. As fit as a fiddle. The noun is fixed, even though a fiddle and a violin are the same instrument.
  • My boss is as stubborn as a donkey. As stubborn as a mule. A literal translation (e.g. from German) is understood, but English uses mule.
  • The new intern is as quiet as a mice. As quiet as a mouse. The noun is singular with a.
  • Our CEO is as sharp as a tack, as busy as a bee and as cool as a cucumber. Choose one simile. Stacking cliches sounds like a parody or a weak reference letter.

The big idea

A simile compares two things openly with as or like; a metaphor simply says one thing is another. He's as stubborn as a mule is a simile; he's a mule is a metaphor, and it sounds much ruder.

The as + adjective + as + noun frame works as an intensifier: as proud as a peacock just means extremely proud, with a picture attached. Because the picture does the work, the noun is usually fixed by tradition, and changing it sounds odd.

Many of these similes are old and slightly folksy, so they're best in conversation, stories and light writing. In casual speech the first as often disappears: cool as a cucumber, fit as a fiddle.

You already use it:

  • My aunt has a memory like an elephant; she never forgets a birthday.
  • He hardly touched his dinner. He eats like a bird.
  • When they called his name he went as red as a beetroot.
  • She was shaking like a leaf before her driving test.
  • You look like a million bucks in that suit.
  • Our coach has eyes like a hawk; nothing gets past her.
  • Grandpa was like a kid in a candy store at the hardware show.

a muleas stubborn as a muleDad is as stubborn as a mule; he still won't use a GPS.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: extremely unwilling to change your mind or behaviour casual

Bend it: Often shortened: stubborn as a mule. Also mule-headed. Usually critical, but can be affectionate in families.

Note: Trap: not as stubborn as a donkey or as a goat. Near-synonym pig-headed is ruder.

a mouseas quiet as a mouseThe new analyst was as quiet as a mouse for her first month.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: very quiet, shy or making almost no noise neutral

Bend it: Describes people or how they move: she crept up the stairs as quiet as a mouse. Also mousy (shy and dull, unkind).

Note: Neutral or kind, unlike mousy. Don't confuse with as poor as a church mouse, which is about money and old-fashioned.

a peacockas proud as a peacockHe walked around the wedding as proud as a peacock after his speech.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: very proud, often showing off humorous

Bend it: Fixed noun. Also the verb to peacock (show off) and preen. Often lightly mocking.

Note: Can be warm (a proud parent) or critical (vanity). Context and tone of voice decide.

a cucumberas cool as a cucumberThe pilot stayed as cool as a cucumber during the emergency landing.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: very calm and relaxed, especially under pressure casual

Bend it: Very often without the first as: cool as a cucumber. Always a cucumber.

Note: Admiring. About calm, not about being fashionable. Near-synonym: calm, cool and collected.

a fiddle (a violin)as fit as a fiddleAfter the hip replacement, Mum is as fit as a fiddle again.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: in very good health, often said of older people casual

Bend it: Fit as a fiddle in casual speech. Usually after be, feel or look.

Note: A slightly old-fashioned, cheerful phrase. Fit once meant suitable or in good condition, like a well-tuned fiddle.

a beeas busy as a beeWith three grandchildren visiting, I've been as busy as a bee all week.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: very busy, working actively and cheerfully casual

Bend it: Related noun: a busy bee (she's a real busy bee). Not as busy like a bee.

Note: Positive: productive and energetic. If you mean stressed and overloaded, swamped or snowed under is better.

a tack (a drawing pin)as sharp as a tackOur MP is as sharp as a tack; she remembers every figure.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: very intelligent and quick-thinking casual

Bend it: Mainly North American. British speakers say as sharp as a knife or as sharp as a razor; razor-sharp works everywhere.

Note: Often used for older people who are still mentally quick: at 95 he's still sharp as a tack. A tack is a small pin.

a clam (a shellfish)as happy as a clamGive him a lawn chair and a fishing rod and he's as happy as a clam.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: very happy and contented casual

Bend it: North American. The British and Australian version is as happy as Larry. Also happy as a pig in mud (very informal).

Note: Originally longer: happy as a clam at high tide, when it's safe from diggers. The short form is what people say now.

In literature, poetry and music

Writers and songwriters use the same big idea. Read about each one, then find it and read or listen together in the lesson.

Poetry

A Red, Red Rose

Robert Burns, 1794

Burns opens with two famous like similes: his love is compared to a rose newly opened in early summer and to a sweetly played tune. He then promises to love her until the seas dry up. It's the classic example of a simile used for a person.

If you had to compare someone you love to a flower or a piece of music, which would you choose and why?

Literature

The Canterbury Tales: General Prologue

Geoffrey Chaucer, c. 1387-1400

Chaucer introduces each pilgrim with quick comparisons. The young Squire is said to be as fresh as the month of May, while the brawny Miller has a beard as red as a sow or a fox. Six hundred years ago, English already described people this way.

Describe someone you know in one simile, the way Chaucer does. Is it kind or mocking?

Literature

Alice's Adventures in Wonderland

Lewis Carroll, 1865

The Hatter and the March Hare host the famous mad tea party. Carroll didn't invent as mad as a hatter or as mad as a March hare: the hare simile goes back to the early 1500s, and the Nova Scotian writer Thomas Chandler Haliburton used the hatter one in The Clockmaker in the 1830s. Carroll turned both similes into characters.

The hatter link to mercury poisoning is only a theory. Why do you think such origin stories are so popular?

Music

Like a Rolling Stone

Bob Dylan, 1965

The title simile drives the song. Dylan addresses a woman who grew up privileged and looked down on others, and who now has no home and no direction. A rolling stone, from the old proverb about gathering no moss, stands for someone with nothing to hold on to.

Is Dylan's simile sympathetic, cruel or both? What makes you think so?
