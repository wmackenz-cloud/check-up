What this lesson is for

You will be able to

  • recognise irony instantly and respond to it naturally
  • use ironic similes to complain, tease or criticise in a light, indirect way
  • choose a sincere simile instead when irony could be misunderstood

You will know

  • (about) as + positive adjective + as + its opposite
  • eight ironic similes, British and North American

You will understand

  • about + deadpan voice = irony
  • funny with friends, risky with strangers

Common mistakes to watch for

  • The instructions were as clear as mud, so we finished the job in ten minutes. As clear as mud means very unclear. Read the irony: mud is not clear at all.
  • The meeting was as boring as watching the paint drying. About as exciting as watching paint dry. The phrase is fixed; it's also usually ironic (exciting), not boring.
  • This printer is as useful like a chocolate teapot. As useful as a chocolate teapot. The frame is as ... as, never as ... like.
  • We need more paperwork like a fish needs an umbrella. Like a fish needs a bicycle. The famous version is fixed; changing the noun loses the joke.
  • (to your manager) Your plan is as sharp as a bowling ball. Too risky. Use playful insults only about yourself or close friends; at work, say I'm not sure the plan works yet.

The big idea

A sincere simile chooses something that has a lot of the quality: as clear as crystal. An ironic simile does the opposite. It names a positive quality and then compares it to something that completely lacks it: as clear as mud, as useful as a chocolate teapot. The listener's brain notices the clash and gets the joke: the real meaning is the opposite of the adjective.

The little word about is a strong signal: about as useful as, about as exciting as. It sounds like careful understatement, which is exactly what makes it sarcastic. Native speakers deliver these lines deadpan: a flat voice, a straight face, no laugh. The humour comes from pretending to be serious.

Irony is a strong part of British humour and very common in Canada too, but it travels badly. In writing, in a second language, or with people you don't know, it can sound rude or simply be misunderstood. A good rule: be ironic about things, situations and yourself, not about the person in front of you.

You already use it:

  • Getting the kids out the door is like herding cats.
  • Pinning down his position is like nailing jelly to a wall.
  • Another committee? I need that like I need a hole in the head.
  • The second half of the game was like watching grass grow.
  • Affordable apartments downtown are as rare as hen's teeth.
  • That umbrella is about as much use as a chocolate fireguard.

Meaning: very unclear or confusing humorous

Bend it: Often drops the first as: clear as mud. Also self-mocking: I hope that wasn't as clear as mud.

Note: The ironic opposite of as clear as crystal or crystal clear. Safe and friendly: it criticises the explanation, not the person.

chocolate and a teapotas useful as a chocolate teapotThis flimsy little umbrella is about as useful as a chocolate teapot.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: completely useless humorous

Bend it: About as useful as, about as much use as; British variants include a chocolate fireguard.

Note: Mainly British, but understood in Canada. A teapot made of chocolate would melt as soon as you filled it.

a submarine with a screen doorabout as much use as a screen door on a submarineWithout a battery, this smoke detector is about as much use as a screen door on a submarine.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: completely useless, or making things worse humorous

Bend it: Also as useful as a screen door on a submarine; sometimes just a screen door on a submarine.

Note: North American: screen doors (with insect mesh) are a summer cottage staple. Stronger than useless: it would actively sink you.

a sledgehammer (a big, heavy hammer)as subtle as a sledgehammerHis hint that he wanted the corner office was about as subtle as a sledgehammer.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: very obvious and heavy-handed humorous

Bend it: Often with about; also subtle as a brick, as subtle as a punch in the face.

Note: Used for hints, advertising, political messages and films. Mildly critical. A neutral alternative: heavy-handed.

a skunk and a gardenas welcome as a skunk at a garden partyThe tax inspector was about as welcome as a skunk at a garden party.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: extremely unwelcome humorous

Bend it: Often as a metaphor: he was the skunk at the garden party (the one who spoiled the fun).

Note: North American; skunks are a familiar summer nuisance in Ontario. Common in political commentary for an unpopular speaker or bad news.

paint and time passing slowlyabout as exciting as watching paint dryHonestly, the quarterly report was about as exciting as watching paint dry.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: extremely boring humorous

Bend it: Also like watching paint dry and like watching grass grow. Fixed: paint dry, not paint drying.

Note: Very common in both British and North American English. Often used about sport, lectures and long meetings.

a fish and a bicyclelike a fish needs a bicycleThis town needs another parking garage like a fish needs a bicycle.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: not needed at all humorous

Bend it: Usually need X like a fish needs a bicycle. Near-synonym: I need that like I need a hole in the head.

Note: Coined by the Australian writer Irina Dunn in 1970 in a feminist slogan about women and men, and made famous by Gloria Steinem. Now used for anything unnecessary.

a bowling ballas sharp as a bowling ballBefore my first coffee, I'm about as sharp as a bowling ball.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: not clever or quick-thinking at all (playful insult) humorous

Bend it: Also as sharp as a marble; compare the understatement not the sharpest tool in the shed.

Note: Use with care: safest about yourself. Calling a colleague this can be hurtful. The sincere opposite is as sharp as a tack (very clever).

In literature, poetry and music

Writers and songwriters use the same big idea. Read about each one, then find it and read or listen together in the lesson.

Literature

Farewell, My Lovely

Raymond Chandler, 1940

Chandler's detective Philip Marlowe describes Los Angeles in wild, deadpan similes. Early on, a huge ex-convict in loud clothes is said to stand out on a shabby street about as much as a big spider on a pale cake would. The comic exaggeration became the model for hard-boiled crime writing.

Describe someone or something that really stood out in a place, using a Chandler-style simile.

Literature

The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy

Douglas Adams, 1979

When an alien fleet arrives over Earth, the narrator says the enormous ships hang in the sky by comparing them to bricks, which famously do not hang anywhere. It's a simile built on a negative: the joke works because you have to undo the comparison to get it.

Can you invent a negative simile of your own, comparing something to what it is not?

Poetry

Sonnet 130

William Shakespeare, 1609

Love poets of Shakespeare's time compared their ladies' eyes to the sun, lips to coral and skin to snow. In this sonnet the speaker runs through those clichés and admits his lover matches none of them, then says his love is as real as any. It's a parody of tired similes.

Which clichéd compliments or love-song similes would you happily retire today?

Find it
Music

Smells Like Teen Spirit

Nirvana, 1991

The title began as a joke. The musician Kathleen Hanna wrote on Kurt Cobain's wall that he smelled like Teen Spirit, a deodorant his girlfriend wore. Cobain didn't know the brand and took it as a rebellious slogan, so a teasing simile became the title of a generation's anthem.

Do you know other famous names or slogans that began as a joke or a misunderstanding?
