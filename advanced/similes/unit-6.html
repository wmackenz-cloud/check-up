What this lesson is for

Why this matters: Advanced speakers don't only recognise similes; they make them. Knowing the grammar patterns behind similes lets you create fresh, precise comparisons for stories, presentations and writing, and avoid the tired ones.

You will know

  • the main simile patterns: as ... as, like + noun, as if / as though, the way + clause and more ... than
  • what extended and epic (Homeric) similes are and where they come from

You will understand

  • the difference between a simile, which compares openly, and a metaphor, which says one thing is another
  • why a fresh simile is specific and surprising, while a cliché is predictable

You will be able to

  • build your own similes with five different grammar patterns
  • develop a simple comparison into an extended simile in speech or writing
  • refresh a cliché by twisting, extending or contradicting it

Common mistakes to watch for

  • The office was as quiet like a library. As quiet as a library. The as ... as frame needs the second as.
  • She sang as a bird at the wedding. She sang like a bird. As a bird means in the role of a bird.
  • He looked at me like I was a ghost, in his report to the board. In formal writing, prefer as if I were a ghost. Like + clause is fine in casual speech.
  • The hall was more quiet as a church. The hall was quieter than a church. Comparatives take than.
  • Just as the river carves the valley, also patience shapes a career. Just as ..., so patience shapes a career. The epic pattern is just as ... so ....

The big idea

A simile compares two unlike things openly, with a signal word: like, as, as if, the way, than. A metaphor drops the signal and says one thing is another. A simile is a little more modest and more precise: it says in this one way, these two things are alike.

A simile becomes a cliché when the second half is completely predictable: if you say as busy as a ..., everyone thinks bee. A fresh simile is concrete, specific and slightly surprising, but instantly clear: as busy as a phone charger at an airport. The test is simple: can the listener picture it, and did they not see it coming?

Similes can also grow. An extended simile develops one comparison over several clauses. An epic or Homeric simile, named after the Greek poet Homer, turns the comparison into a small scene of its own, often in the pattern just as ..., so .... You'll hear shorter versions in speeches, sermons and sports commentary.

You already use it:

  • The traffic this morning was like a parking lot.
  • He talks to his car as if it were a person.
  • She's as stubborn as a mule, just like her mother.
  • Their new house is bigger than a hotel.
  • The kids ran off like a shot when the bell rang.
  • It feels as though summer lasted a week.

The expressions

as + adjective + asAfter the funeral the house was as quiet as a held breath.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: compares one quality to something that has a lot of it neutral

Bend it: Negative: not as tidy as a hotel. Intensified: just as, twice as. Casual speech drops the first as: quiet as a mouse.

Note: The easiest pattern to refresh: keep the adjective, change the noun. Trap: never as ... like.

like + nounThe rumour went through the office like a cold through a daycare.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: compares an action or a thing to a picture casual

Bend it: After almost any verb: run like, look like, spread like. Add detail to make it fresh: like a cold through a daycare rather than like wildfire.

Note: Like + noun compares; as + noun gives a role (she worked as a nurse). This is the most common source of simile errors.

as if / as though + clauseHe shook my hand as if he were signing a peace treaty.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: compares with an imagined situation neutral

Bend it: Formal: as if he were; everyday: as if he was; casual North American: like he was.

Note: As if and as though mean the same. Use the past tense (or were) for something unreal; the present for something possibly true: it looks as if it's going to rain.

the way + clauseShe carried the folder the way you'd carry a newborn baby.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: compares how two actions are done neutral

Bend it: Often with you or someone: the way you'd ..., the way a cat watches a bird. The way that is possible but heavier.

Note: A quiet, elegant pattern favoured by novelists. It compares manner, so it suits actions and gestures better than qualities.

more ... thanThat budget meeting was more painful than a root canal.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: compares by saying something goes beyond a familiar extreme humorous

Bend it: Comparative adjectives: quieter than a library, colder than a landlord's heart; also less ... than.

Note: Only a simile when the things are unlike: taller than his brother is just a comparison. Often humorous exaggeration.

extended simileStarting a business is like planting an orchard: you dig, you water, you wait for years, and one late frost can take the lot.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: a simile developed over several clauses or sentences neutral

Bend it: Usually starts with a short simile, then adds a colon or you ... you ... to develop it. Keep every detail inside the same picture.

Note: Powerful in presentations and speeches. Trap: switching to a new image halfway creates a mixed metaphor.

epic (Homeric) simileJust as a farmer watches the sky all week for hail, so the finance minister watched the markets before the budget.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: a long, detailed comparison that becomes a small scene of its own formal

Bend it: Pattern: just as ..., so ... or like ..., so .... Can run to several sentences in literature.

Note: Named after Homer's Iliad and Odyssey. In modern use it sounds grand, so it suits speeches, eulogies or deliberate humour.

turning a clichéMy uncle isn't just stubborn as a mule; he's as stubborn as a mule with a lawyer.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: refreshing an old simile by twisting, extending or contradicting it humorous

Bend it: Extend it (a mule with a lawyer), swap the noun (as busy as a bee in a flower shop), or deny it (far from cool as a cucumber, he was a pickle in a heatwave).

Note: Native listeners enjoy the surprise because they know the original. Do it once per conversation; more sounds like showing off.

In literature, poetry and music

Writers and songwriters use the same big idea. Read about each one, then find it and read or listen together in the lesson.

Literature

The Iliad

Homer, c. 8th century BC

Homer's war epic is full of long similes that pause the battle to show a scene from ordinary life: warriors like lions attacking a flock, armies like waves. In one famous passage, the warrior Glaucus compares the generations of men to the leaves of a tree, which fall and grow again each spring.

Glaucus compares human generations to leaves. What image would you use for generations in your family?

Find it
Poetry

The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock

T. S. Eliot, 1915

Eliot's poem opens with one of the most startling similes in modern poetry: the evening sky is compared not to something beautiful but to a hospital patient lying unconscious on an operating table. Readers expecting a romantic sunset get something cold and clinical instead, and that shock sets the nervous, uneasy mood of the whole poem.

Describe a sunset, a city street or a Monday morning with a simile that surprises the listener instead of the one they expect.

Find it
Poetry

I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud

William Wordsworth, 1807

The title itself is a simile: the speaker compares himself, walking alone, to a single cloud drifting over hills and valleys, until he comes across a crowd of daffodils by a lake. The simile sets the mood of solitude that the flowers then break. Compare Langston Hughes's Harlem, a whole poem built from similes.

Make a simile for how you feel when you walk alone somewhere you love.

Find it
Music

Like a Prayer

Madonna, 1989

The title simile compares the feeling of hearing someone's voice, and of being close to them, to the experience of prayer. By borrowing religious language for love, Madonna made an ordinary pop comparison feel bold, and the song and its video caused a major controversy when they came out.

What would you compare a powerful experience, like falling in love or hearing great news, to? Try a fresh image from an unexpected area of life.
