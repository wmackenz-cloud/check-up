Quick reminder

A sincere simile chooses something that has a lot of the quality: as clear as crystal. An ironic simile does the opposite. It names a positive quality and then compares it to something that completely lacks it: as clear as mud, as useful as a chocolate teapot. The listener's brain notices the clash and gets the joke: the real meaning is the opposite of the adjective.

  • as clear as mud: very unclear or confusing
  • as useful as a chocolate teapot: completely useless
  • about as much use as a screen door on a submarine: completely useless, or making things worse
  • as subtle as a sledgehammer: very obvious and heavy-handed
  • as welcome as a skunk at a garden party: extremely unwelcome
  • about as exciting as watching paint dry: extremely boring
  • like a fish needs a bicycle: not needed at all
  • as sharp as a bowling ball: not clever or quick-thinking at all (playful insult)

Watch out for

  • The instructions were as clear as mud, so we finished the job in ten minutes. As clear as mud means very unclear. Read the irony: mud is not clear at all.
  • The meeting was as boring as watching the paint drying. About as exciting as watching paint dry. The phrase is fixed; it's also usually ironic (exciting), not boring.
  • This printer is as useful like a chocolate teapot. As useful as a chocolate teapot. The frame is as ... as, never as ... like.

  • like nailing jelly to a wall = impossible to pin down
