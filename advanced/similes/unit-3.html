What this lesson is for

You will be able to

  • describe habits, success and speed with natural action similes
  • change tense and subject freely while keeping the fixed noun
  • judge register, from neutral (spread like wildfire) to slangy (like a bat out of hell)

You will know

  • verb + like + noun
  • sell like hot cakes, spread like wildfire

You will understand

  • like, not as, before a noun
  • praise (fit like a glove) vs criticism (drink like a fish)

Common mistakes to watch for

  • The rumour spread as wildfire through the office. The rumour spread like wildfire. Before a noun after a verb, use like; as means in the role of.
  • Her new novel is selling like hot bread. Selling like hot cakes (or hotcakes). A literal translation (e.g. French petits pains) sounds odd in English.
  • I slept like a stone after the hike. I slept like a log. English also says slept like a baby, but not a stone.
  • The wedding went as a clockwork. The wedding went like clockwork. No article: clockwork is uncountable.
  • He took to golf like a fish to water. He took to golf like a duck to water. The animal is fixed.

The big idea

After a verb, English uses like + noun to compare how something happens: she sleeps like a log, it fits like a glove. Using as here changes the meaning: he worked as a horse would mean that was his job.

Action similes package a whole picture into one phrase. Spread like wildfire tells you the speed, the danger and the impossibility of stopping it. That's why journalists and commentators love them.

The verb and the noun usually come as a pair. Things sell like hot cakes, rumours spread like wildfire, plans go or run like clockwork. Change the tense or the subject, but keep the pair together.

You already use it:

  • My brother drives like a maniac on the 401.
  • The choir's soprano sings like an angel.
  • The news of the layoffs hit me like a train.
  • We worked like dogs all summer to finish the cottage.
  • As soon as the bell rang, the kids took off like a shot.
  • The new manager watches us like a hawk.
  • After the long shift I went out like a light.

Meaning: sleep very deeply and without waking casual

Bend it: Any tense: I slept like a log, you'll sleep like a log. Plural: slept like logs.

Note: Near-synonyms: sleep like a baby (peacefully), go out like a light (fall asleep instantly). Not like a stone.

a horseeat like a horseOur teenage son eats like a horse; we buy milk every two days.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: eat very large amounts casual

Bend it: Usually present simple for habits. Opposite: eat like a bird (very little).

Note: Fine for family and friends; saying it to or about a woman you don't know well may sound rude.

wildfire (a fire spreading through a forest)spread like wildfireThe video of the moose in the mall spread like wildfire online.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: spread very quickly and uncontrollably neutral

Bend it: Used with news, rumours, panic, trends and diseases. Any tense. Not spread like fire.

Note: Neutral enough for news reports. In Canada and California the literal image is very real, so it carries extra force.

hot cakes (pancakes)sell like hot cakesHeat pumps are selling like hot cakes since the rebate was announced.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: sell very quickly and in large numbers casual

Bend it: Also spelled hotcakes. Usually progressive or past: they're selling / they sold like hot cakes.

Note: Hot cakes are pancakes, which sold fast at fairs. Don't translate a local version like hot bread.

a glovefit like a gloveThe suit I found at the thrift store fits like a glove.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: fit perfectly; be exactly right neutral

Bend it: For clothes, but also jobs and roles: the new role fits her like a glove. Add an object: fits me like a glove.

Note: Always positive. Compare suit someone down to the ground (British): be perfect for someone.

a charm (a lucky object, like a four-leaf clover)work like a charmI tried turning it off and on again, and it worked like a charm.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: work perfectly, often better than expected casual

Bend it: Usually past simple: it worked like a charm. Variant: work like a dream (British leaning).

Note: A charm is a magic spell, so the idea is that it worked almost magically. About methods and tricks, not people working hard.

clockwork (the gears inside a clock)go like clockworkThanks to the volunteers, the marathon went like clockwork.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: happen exactly as planned, with no problems neutral

Bend it: Go or run like clockwork. Related: as regular as clockwork (happening at the same time every time).

Note: No article: not like a clockwork. Good in work emails and reports.

a duck and watertake to something like a duck to waterMy mother took to her tablet like a duck to water.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: learn or adapt to something naturally and easily casual

Bend it: Pattern: take to + noun/ing + like a duck to water. Usually past: she took to teaching like a duck to water.

Note: The verb is take to (begin to like or do well). Not like a fish to water. Always positive and admiring.

In literature, poetry and music

Writers and songwriters use the same big idea. Read about each one, then find it and read or listen together in the lesson.

Literature

The Trial

Franz Kafka, 1925

Josef K. is arrested one morning and spends the novel fighting a court that never tells him his crime. At the very end, as he is put to death, his final thought is that he is dying like a dog, and he feels the shame will outlive him. Two words of action simile sum up a whole life without dignity.

English has many <em>like a dog</em> similes: work like a dog, be treated like a dog, die like a dog. Why do you think dogs appear in so many negative action similes?

Poetry

The Eagle

Alfred, Lord Tennyson, 1851

This six-line poem shows an eagle perched high on a cliff above the sea, watching. In the final line the bird drops, and Tennyson compares its sudden dive to a thunderbolt. One action simile carries all the speed and power of the poem.

What action in sport or nature would you describe with a thunderbolt simile? Why?

Find it
Music

Like a Hurricane

Neil Young, 1977

The Canadian songwriter compares the overwhelming, uncontrollable feeling of being drawn to someone to being caught in a hurricane. The long guitar solos make the storm audible. Like spread like wildfire, it pictures a force you can't resist.

Which is the better image for falling in love: a hurricane, wildfire, or something calmer? Why?

Listen to it
Music

Like a Rock

Bob Seger, 1986

Seger looks back at himself as a strong, steady young man and compares him to a rock, then wonders where that strength went. The song later became famous in Chevrolet truck commercials, where the simile sold toughness and reliability.

Is it fair for advertisers to use a song about lost youth to sell trucks? What changes when a simile becomes a slogan?
