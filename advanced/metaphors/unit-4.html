What this lesson is for

Why this matters: English describes life as a voyage across water: calm or stormy, shallow or deep. These idioms let you talk precisely about risk, struggle, change and letting go.

You will know

  • the conceptual metaphor LIFE IS A JOURNEY BY WATER and its link to the wider LIFE IS A JOURNEY
  • eight common idioms of water, depth, tides and boats

You will understand

  • the difference between being in deep water and being in hot water
  • why some idioms describe struggle, some describe progress and some describe change

You will be able to

  • describe difficulty, caution and turning points with idiomatic precision
  • choose between close pairs such as tread water and keep your head above water
  • talk about the past and moving on with water under the bridge

Common mistakes to watch for

  • After the scandal, the minister is in hot water financially. The minister is in deep water financially. Hot water means trouble for doing wrong; deep water means serious difficulty.
  • That argument is water below the bridge. That argument is water under the bridge. The preposition is fixed.
  • I felt out of my deep at the new job. I felt out of my depth. The noun is depth.
  • Let's test the water of the market first. Let's test the waters first. The idiom is usually plural and takes no of.
  • Don't shake the boat, the deal is almost done. Don't rock the boat. Only rock works here.

The big idea

Lakoff and Johnson showed that English often treats LIFE IS A JOURNEY. When that journey is by water, we get a rich family of idioms: we navigate a career, steer a company, drift apart, and hope for plain sailing.

Water stands for forces we can't fully control. Its depth measures difficulty (out of your depth, in deep water), its movement stands for trends and change (go with the flow, turn the tide), and storms are crises we must weather.

For Canadians, with three oceans and the Great Lakes, these images are not abstract. The literal danger of water is what gives the metaphors their force.

You already use it:

  • We need someone to steer the project through the next phase.
  • After university the two friends slowly drifted apart.
  • The company is navigating a difficult market.
  • There's a wave of resignations across the sector.
  • The economy is finally on an even keel again.
  • She was flooded with messages after the interview.
  • I'm drowning in paperwork this month.
  • Public opinion is flowing in the other direction now.

The expressions

test the watersThe senator is testing the waters for a leadership run.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: try something carefully to see how people react before committing neutral

Bend it: Usually plural waters; test the water is also heard in Britain. Often test the waters for + noun.

Note: Very common in politics and business. Near-synonym: dip a toe in the water, which suggests a small first step.

out of your depthIn the meeting with the tax lawyers, I felt completely out of my depth.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: in a situation that is too difficult for your knowledge or ability neutral

Bend it: Change the possessive: out of her depth, way out of our depth. Usually with be or feel.

Note: From swimming where your feet can't touch the bottom. It's about ability, not about having too much work.

keep your head above waterWith rent rising, many young families are struggling to keep their heads above water.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: manage to survive, especially financially, but only just neutral

Bend it: Just about keep our heads above water, struggle to keep your head above water. Plural heads with plural subjects.

Note: Focus is on survival. Compare tread water, which focuses on making no progress.

tread waterThe negotiations have been treading water since the spring.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: stay in the same position without making progress neutral

Bend it: Any tense: we're just treading water, the economy trod water (or treaded). No article before water.

Note: Usually mildly negative: you are busy but going nowhere.

turn the tideA late goal from the captain turned the tide in the final period.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: change a situation, usually from bad to good neutral

Bend it: Turn the tide against/in favour of; also the tide is turning, stem the tide (stop a bad trend).

Note: Popular with sports commentators and historians. Watch the difference: turn the tide changes direction, stem the tide only stops it.

rock the boatHe had doubts about the policy but didn't want to rock the boat before his review.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: cause trouble by challenging a stable situation casual

Bend it: Usually negative: don't rock the boat, I didn't want to rock the boat. The wording is fixed.

Note: Can praise or criticise: someone who never rocks the boat may be loyal or simply timid.

water under the bridgeWe fought a lot as teenagers, but that's all water under the bridge now.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: past problems that are forgotten or no longer matter casual

Bend it: Usually it's (all) water under the bridge. A fixed phrase: don't change the preposition.

Note: Signals forgiveness. Close to bury the hatchet, but that is an action; this describes the past.

in deep waterIf the loan isn't approved by Friday, the business will be in deep water.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: in serious difficulty neutral

Bend it: Get into deep water, be in deep water. Not usually plural.

Note: Trap: in hot water means in trouble because you did something wrong and someone is angry. Deep water is just serious danger.

In literature, poetry and music

Writers and songwriters use the same big idea. Read about each one, then find it and read or listen together in the lesson.

Literature

The Tempest

William Shakespeare, c. 1611

The spirit Ariel sings to Ferdinand, who believes his father has drowned, describing how the sea has transformed the body into coral and pearls. This is where sea change comes from; today it means a complete, deep transformation.

What area of society has gone through a sea change in your lifetime?

Find it
Literature

Julius Caesar

William Shakespeare, 1599

Before the final battle, Brutus argues that opportunity in human life works like the tide: catch it at its height and it carries you to success; miss it and you may be stranded in the shallows for the rest of your life. It is the classic picture behind turn the tide.

Have you ever caught, or missed, a high tide in your own life?

Find it
Poetry

Not Waving but Drowning

Stevie Smith, 1957

A man dies in the sea and onlookers had assumed he was just waving and having fun. The poet turns this into a picture of someone who has struggled to keep their head above water all their life while everyone thought they were fine.

Why do people sometimes hide the fact that they are out of their depth?

Find it
Music

Bridge over Troubled Water

Simon & Garfunkel, 1970

Paul Simon's song uses the title image as a promise of loyalty: when a friend's life becomes dangerous and stormy, the singer offers to become the safe way across. Troubled water stands for any hard time.

Who has been a bridge over troubled water for you, or for whom have you been one?

Listen to it
Music

The Wreck of the Edmund Fitzgerald

Gordon Lightfoot, 1976

The Canadian singer tells the true story of a freighter that sank in a storm on Lake Superior in November 1975, with all 29 crew lost. Here water is not a metaphor at all, and the song reminds us of the real danger behind phrases like weather the storm.

Does the literal danger of water make these idioms feel stronger to you? Which ones?
