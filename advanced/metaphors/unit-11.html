  • fixed animal characters (may differ in your language)
  • animal labels: insulting or humorous

Common mistakes to watch for

  • She let the dog out of the bag. She let the cat out of the bag. The animal is fixed.
  • He cried tears of crocodile. He shed crocodile tears. Crocodile works as an adjective, before the noun.
  • The boss took the share of the lion. The boss took the lion's share. Use the possessive, not of the.
  • There's an elephant in the room: by the way, how was your holiday? Use it only for a serious, avoided problem, then actually name or address it.
  • My brother is the black sheep: he's a doctor and everyone's proud of him. The black sheep is the family member seen as a disappointment, not just someone different.

The big idea

People are often described as animals, and each animal carries a fixed character: foxes are cunning, wolves are dangerous or solitary, sheep follow the crowd, lions are powerful. Most of these images come from fables, farming and the Bible, so they are old and stable.

A second group is about hidden meaning: secrets kept in bags, enormous animals nobody mentions, smelly fish that lead you off the trail. These idioms let speakers point at awkward truths without saying them directly.

Because animal labels judge character, they are rarely neutral. Calling a colleague a snake or a politician a dinosaur is a strong move. Check whether the animal means the same thing in your language: an owl is wise in English but unlucky in some cultures.

You already use it:

  • He wolfed down his lunch in five minutes.
  • Stop badgering me about the report.
  • The senator weaselled out of answering the question.
  • She's been hounded by reporters all week.
  • Investors flocked to the new fund.
  • Don't just parrot the textbook; give your own view.
  • The journalist ferreted out the truth from old tax records.
  • A few sheepish employees admitted they hadn't read the policy.

the elephant in the roomNobody at the family dinner mentioned Dad's drinking, but it was the elephant in the room.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: an obvious, important problem that everyone avoids mentioning neutral

Bend it: Flexible: address the elephant in the room, a huge elephant in the room, even a whole herd of elephants as a joke.

Note: Very common in business and politics, so some editors call it overused. Address or acknowledge the elephant is the usual verb.

a red herringThe fuss about the mayor's haircut was a red herring; the real issue was the budget.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: something that distracts attention from the real issue, often deliberately neutral

Bend it: Fixed noun phrase, but plurals work: the novel is full of red herrings.

Note: A favourite term in mystery fiction for a false clue. Its exact origin is debated, so treat the popular dog-training story with caution.

a dark horseNobody expected the quiet candidate from Saskatchewan to win; she was a dark horse.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: a little-known competitor who unexpectedly succeeds, or someone with surprising hidden talents neutral

Bend it: Used as an adjective: a dark-horse candidate. British speakers also say You're a dark horse! to someone with a surprising secret.

Note: Popularised by Benjamin Disraeli's novel The Young Duke (1831), in which an unknown horse wins a race. Never black horse.

let the cat out of the bagMy nephew let the cat out of the bag about the surprise party.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: reveal a secret, usually by accident casual

Bend it: Flexible: the cat's out of the bag now, who let the cat out?

Note: Usually accidental. For a deliberate leak to the press, leak or blow the whistle is more precise.

the lion's shareThe province's two largest cities receive the lion's share of the funding.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: the largest part of something neutral

Bend it: Almost fixed: the lion's share of. Singular lion, with an apostrophe before the s.

Note: From an Aesop fable in which the lion keeps all the prey for himself. Originally it meant all of it; today it usually means the biggest part.

a sacred cowThe new CEO says no department is a sacred cow in the budget review.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: a belief, custom or institution treated as beyond criticism neutral

Bend it: Plurals are common: challenge sacred cows, slaughter a few sacred cows.

Note: Inspired by the reverence for cows in Hinduism. Used about politics and business; be thoughtful using it in conversations about religion itself.

crocodile tearsThe executives shed crocodile tears over the layoffs while accepting huge bonuses.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: fake sadness or sympathy dismissive

Bend it: Usually with shed or weep: shedding crocodile tears. Always plural.

Note: From an old legend that crocodiles weep while eating their prey. It is a strong accusation of hypocrisy.

a wolf in sheep's clothingThe friendly investment adviser turned out to be a wolf in sheep's clothing.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: a dangerous person who appears harmless neutral

Bend it: Plural works: wolves in sheep's clothing. The rest is fixed.

Note: The image appears in the Gospel of Matthew, warning against false prophets, and in later fables attributed to Aesop.

In literature, poetry and music

Writers and songwriters use the same big idea. Read about each one, then find it and read or listen together in the lesson.

Literature

Aesop's Fables

attributed to Aesop, ancient Greece (traditionally 6th century BCE)

Short stories in which animals behave like people: a lion who keeps all the prey, a wolf disguised in a fleece, a fox who calls the grapes he can't reach sour. Each animal is a fixed human type, and English inherited idioms such as the lion's share and sour grapes from them.

Is there a fable or folk tale from your culture whose moral became a common saying?

Literature

Animal Farm

George Orwell, 1945

Farm animals overthrow their human farmer, but the pigs slowly become new tyrants. The whole novel is an extended metaphor, an allegory, for the Russian Revolution and Stalin's rule, with each animal standing for a type of person or group.

Why might a writer tell a political story with animals instead of people? What does it allow him to say?

Find it
Poetry

Hope is the thing with feathers

Emily Dickinson, written around 1861

Dickinson pictures hope as a small bird living inside a person, singing without stopping through the worst storms and never asking for anything in return. An abstract feeling becomes a creature you can almost hold.

If you described courage, worry or love as an animal, which animal would it be?

Find it
Music

Roar

Katy Perry, 2013

The singer describes moving from being quiet and pushed around to finding her strength. The tiger and its roar become a metaphor for confidence regained, a modern pop version of the lion as a symbol of courage.

Which animal would you choose as a symbol of confidence in your culture? Is it the same as in English?

Listen to it
Literature

The Metamorphosis

Franz Kafka, 1915

A travelling salesman wakes up transformed into a huge insect, and his family slowly turns away from him. Readers often see the change as a metaphor for alienation, illness or being treated as useless once you can no longer earn money.

Have you ever felt like a different creature in a new job, school or country? What changed in how people treated you?
