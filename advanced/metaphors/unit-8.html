  • Discuss a poem, novel or song that presents life as a road or a climb.

You will know

  • at a crossroads, go the extra mile

You will understand

  • goals = destinations, difficulties = obstacles
  • The Road Not Taken: often misread

Common mistakes to watch for

  • We'll cross that bridge when we arrive to it. We'll cross that bridge when we come to it. The idiom is fixed.
  • She went the extra kilometre for her clients. She went the extra mile. Even in metric Canada, the idiom keeps mile.
  • I'm in a crossroads in my career. I'm at a crossroads in my career. We stand at a point on a road.
  • You're in the right track. You're on the right track. Trains and runners are on a track.
  • We hit a dead end, but there's light at the end of the bridge. There's light at the end of the tunnel. Mixing roads, bridges and tunnels confuses the image.

The big idea

In English, progress is movement along a path. A goal is a destination, a plan is a route, problems are obstacles, choices are forks in the road, and how far we have got is distance travelled.

That is why we say a career is going nowhere, a project is on track or behind schedule, a couple has drifted apart, and a country is moving forward. Even 'progress' itself comes from a Latin word for stepping forward.

The metaphor also carries values: forward is good, going back is bad, and difficult roads are uphill. Noticing this helps you see why some political slogans and business mission statements feel persuasive.

You already use it:

  • We've reached an important milestone: our hundredth client.
  • The talks hit a roadblock over pension funding.
  • I got sidetracked by emails and never finished the report.
  • Nobody knows what the road ahead holds for the industry.
  • She chose a route off the beaten track and studied ancient languages.
  • After the merger, we came to a fork in the road.
  • The negotiations are finally moving forward.
  • Their relationship seems to be going nowhere.

Meaning: a situation or path with no possibility of progress neutral

Bend it: As adjective: a dead-end job, a dead-end relationship. Verb phrases: hit / reach a dead end.

Note: A dead-end job is a strong phrase: no promotion, no future. It can sound insulting about someone else's work.

Meaning: a struggle that is very hard to win neutral

Bend it: Also an uphill struggle / climb / fight. Verb phrase: face an uphill battle.

Note: Mixes journey (uphill) and war (battle). The image is soldiers fighting up a slope, always at a disadvantage.

Meaning: doing things in a way that will probably succeed neutral

Bend it: Opposite: on the wrong track. Related: back on track, off track, keep on track.

Note: Encouraging and very useful for teachers and managers giving feedback. On track alone means 'on schedule'.

Meaning: a sign that a long difficult period is ending neutral

Bend it: Often see / there's light at the end of the tunnel. Joke version: the light at the end of the tunnel is an oncoming train.

Note: Hopeful but cautious: the difficulty isn't over yet. Politicians use it often, so it can sound like spin.

cross that bridge when we come to itWhat if the venue cancels? We'll cross that bridge when we come to it.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: deal with a problem only when it actually happens casual

Bend it: Pronouns change: I'll / you can cross that bridge when you come to it. Short form: that's a bridge to cross later.

Note: Can sound relaxed and wise, or like avoiding planning. Don't confuse with burn your bridges (destroy relationships).

go the extra mileOur contractor went the extra mile and fixed the fence for free.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: make more effort than is expected neutral

Bend it: Tense changes: going the extra mile. Fixed noun: always the extra mile, never kilometre.

Note: From the Gospel of Matthew, where Jesus tells listeners to go two miles when someone forces them to go one. Common in job ads and reviews.

Meaning: make a lot of progress neutral

Bend it: Usually present perfect: you've come a long way. Add since or from: come a long way from her first job.

Note: Warm praise, but in some contexts it can sound patronizing, as if the person started very low. Still have a long way to go is the opposite.

In literature, poetry and music

Writers and songwriters use the same big idea. Read about each one, then find it and read or listen together in the lesson.

Poetry

The Road Not Taken

Robert Frost, 1916

A traveller chooses between two paths in a wood, though the poem says they are worn about equally. He imagines telling the story years later as if his choice changed everything. It is often read as a call to be different, but many critics see a gentle irony: we invent meaning for our choices afterwards. Frost reportedly had his indecisive friend, the poet Edward Thomas, in mind.

Do you think people exaggerate how important their past choices were? Think of a 'road not taken' in your own life.

Poetry

Mother to Son

Langston Hughes, 1922

A mother tells her son that her life has not been a smooth, elegant staircase but a rough, broken one, full of hazards and dark stretches. Still she has kept climbing, and she urges him not to stop. Progress here is a hard climb, not a road.

Who in your family 'kept climbing' through difficult times? What did they teach you?

Literature

The Pilgrim's Progress

John Bunyan, 1678

This Christian allegory follows a man named Christian on a journey from the City of Destruction to the Celestial City. On the way he falls into the Slough of Despond and passes through Vanity Fair, place names that became English expressions. Every spiritual difficulty is a physical obstacle on the road.

If your life were an allegory, what would you call its places: the swamp, the fair, the hill?

Find it
Music

The Long and Winding Road

The Beatles (written by Paul McCartney), 1970

From the album Let It Be, the song describes a long, winding road that keeps leading the singer back to the person he loves. The journey is not about career success but about longing and return, and the endless curves suggest frustration.

Is there a 'long and winding road' in your life: a goal that kept bringing you back after detours?

Listen to it
Music

Road to Nowhere

Talking Heads, 1985

A cheerful, marching song about travelling on a road that leads nowhere in particular. Instead of treating that as failure, the singer seems to accept it with a smile. It flips the usual metaphor: maybe life is a journey without a destination.

Is it comforting or depressing to think life has no final destination? Explain your view.
