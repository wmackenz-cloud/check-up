Quick reminder

Lakoff and Johnson showed that English often treats LIFE IS A JOURNEY. When that journey is by water, we get a rich family of idioms: we navigate a career, steer a company, drift apart, and hope for plain sailing.

  • test the waters: try something carefully to see how people react before committing
  • out of your depth: in a situation that is too difficult for your knowledge or ability
  • keep your head above water: manage to survive, especially financially, but only just
  • tread water: stay in the same position without making progress
  • turn the tide: change a situation, usually from bad to good
  • rock the boat: cause trouble by challenging a stable situation
  • water under the bridge: past problems that are forgotten or no longer matter
  • in deep water: in serious difficulty

Watch out for

  • After the business failed, the family was in hot water financially. The family was in deep water financially. Hot water means trouble for doing wrong; deep water means serious difficulty.
  • That argument is water below the bridge. That argument is water under the bridge. The preposition is fixed.
  • I felt out of my deep at the new job. I felt out of my depth. The noun is depth.
