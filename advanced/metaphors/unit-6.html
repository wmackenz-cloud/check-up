What this lesson is for

Why this matters: Meetings, emails and news reports are full of sports images: balls, goalposts, plates and towels. They are quick and vivid, but many come from baseball or cricket, so knowing which side of the Atlantic they belong to helps you choose the right one.

You will know

  • Eight core sports idioms for work, such as drop the ball, move the goalposts and step up to the plate.
  • Which idioms come from baseball (North American) and which from football, cricket or boxing (more British or international).

You will understand

  • How the metaphor turns work into a game with rules, teams, referees and a score.
  • Why many of these idioms are really about fairness: who changed the rules, who hit below the belt.

You will be able to

  • Describe a workplace problem or success using sports idioms in a natural, not overdone, way.
  • Choose a North American or British idiom to suit your listener.
  • Discuss a song, poem or book that links sport with success, failure or fairness.

Common mistakes to watch for

  • The ball is on your court now. The ball is in your court now. It is the tennis court; the ball lands in it.
  • They keep moving the goals. They keep moving the goalposts. The idiom names the posts themselves.
  • Can you give me a baseball figure? Can you give me a ballpark figure? A ballpark is the stadium, so a ballpark figure is roughly in the right area.
  • He threw the towel after the third rejection. He threw in the towel. Without in, it only means he threw a towel.
  • We need to step up to the plate, hit it out of the park, touch base and not drop the ball. Use one or two at a time. Stacking sports idioms sounds like a parody of corporate jargon.

The big idea

In English, work is often described as a sport: there are players and teams, rules and referees, winners and losers, and a score to keep. A project is a game, a deadline is the final whistle, a mistake is a dropped ball.

The sports chosen tell you where an idiom grew up. North American business talk is full of baseball (ballpark, step up to the plate, out of left field), while British English leans on football and cricket (move the goalposts, a sticky wicket, it's not cricket). Boxing gives everyone below the belt and throw in the towel.

Because sport depends on rules, these idioms are often about fairness. Complaining that someone moved the goalposts or hit below the belt is a moral judgement wrapped in a game image.

You already use it:

  • Our team scored a big win with the Quebec contract.
  • The two firms are neck and neck in the race for the licence.
  • Let's touch base on Monday about the schedule.
  • Remote work is a whole new ball game for managers.
  • She really hit it out of the park with her keynote speech.
  • Bringing up his divorce in the debate was below the belt.
  • Changing the deadline without warning? That's just not cricket.
  • The new director is calling the shots now.

drop the ballSomeone dropped the ball and the invoices never went out.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: make a mistake by failing to do something you were responsible for casual

Bend it: Tense changes: I dropped the ball on that one. Add the task with on: drop the ball on the renewal.

Note: A soft way to admit a failure, common in North American offices. 'I dropped the ball' sounds more responsible and less dramatic than 'I failed'.

a level playing fieldThe new rules are meant to create a level playing field for small and large banks.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: a situation where everyone has the same chance neutral

Bend it: Common verbs: create / ensure / provide a level playing field. Opposite: an uneven playing field; verb: level the playing field.

Note: Very common in politics, economics and trade. It is a general sports image, used everywhere in the English-speaking world.

move the goalpostsEvery time we meet the target, head office moves the goalposts.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: unfairly change the rules or targets while something is in progress neutral

Bend it: Often progressive and a complaint: they keep moving the goalposts. Noun: goalpost-moving.

Note: From British football (soccer). Always critical: it accuses someone of unfairness. Canadians and Americans use it too.

the ball's in your courtWe've sent our final offer, so the ball's in their court now.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: it is your turn to act or decide neutral

Bend it: Change the owner: the ball is in Ottawa's court. Also put the ball back in their court.

Note: From tennis. Use in, not on or at. Often used to show that you have done your part.

a ballpark figureI don't need exact numbers, just a ballpark figure for the renovation.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: a rough estimate casual

Bend it: Also in the ballpark (close enough), in the same ballpark (similar), a ballpark estimate.

Note: From baseball; a ballpark is the stadium. Very North American, but now understood in the UK too. Formal alternative: an approximate figure.

out of left fieldThe question about my childhood came completely out of left field.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: unexpected and surprising, often strange casual

Bend it: Fixed core. Add intensifiers: totally / completely out of left field. Also as adjective: a left-field idea.

Note: Baseball, so mainly North American. British speakers more often say out of the blue. Left-field as an adjective can be positive: unconventional, creative.

step up to the plateWhen the manager got sick, Priya stepped up to the plate and ran the launch.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: take responsibility or action when it is needed neutral

Bend it: Shortened to step up. Tense and subject change: it's time for the province to step up to the plate.

Note: Baseball: the batter steps up to home plate. A common call to action in politics. British English may prefer rise to the occasion.

throw in the towelAfter three years of losses, the owners finally threw in the towel and sold the store.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: give up, admit defeat casual

Bend it: Word order can change: throw the towel in. Tense changes. Variant: throw in the sponge (old-fashioned).

Note: From boxing: a trainer throws a towel into the ring to stop the fight. Sounds more final and dramatic than give up.

In literature, poetry and music

Writers and songwriters use the same big idea. Read about each one, then find it and read or listen together in the lesson.

Music

We Are the Champions

Queen (written by Freddie Mercury), 1977

The song looks back on a life of struggles and mistakes and turns it into a victory celebration. Because it is played in stadiums after almost every final, it has become the soundtrack for success as winning a trophy, at work as much as in sport.

Why do you think companies and schools love to borrow the language and music of sports victories?

Music

The Hockey Song

Stompin' Tom Connors, 1973

A cheerful play-by-play of a hockey game that became a Canadian anthem, played in NHL arenas including Ottawa and Toronto. It shows how one sport can stand for a whole national identity, and why Canadians reach for hockey images (a hat trick, a power play, dropping the gloves) at work.

Which sport supplies the metaphors in your first language? Give one example and translate it for Bill.

Listen to it
Poetry

Casey at the Bat

Ernest Lawrence Thayer, 1888

A small-town baseball team's hopes rest on its star batter, who is so confident that he lets good pitches go by and then strikes out. The comic poem is a classic picture of someone who steps up to the plate and fails through arrogance.

Have you seen a 'Casey' at work: someone talented who lost because of overconfidence?

Find it
Literature

Moneyball

Michael Lewis, 2003

This non-fiction book (filmed in 2011) follows the Oakland Athletics, a poor baseball team that used statistics to compete with rich clubs. It became a favourite of business readers because it is about finding ways to play on an uneven playing field.

In your field, how could a small player compete with much bigger, richer rivals?

Find it
Speech

Pre-fight boasts

Muhammad Ali, 1964

Before fighting Sonny Liston for the heavyweight title, Ali (then Cassius Clay) became famous for rhyming boasts. His best-known slogan, usually credited to his assistant Drew Bundini Brown, compared his light footwork to a butterfly and his punches to a bee's sting. Sport talk itself runs on metaphor.

Is confident self-promotion admired or disliked where you grew up? Would Ali's style work in a job interview?
