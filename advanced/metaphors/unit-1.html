Common mistakes to watch for

  • We need to bite the bullet of the price increase. We need to bite the bullet and accept the price increase. Bite the bullet takes no object.
  • She stuck to her weapons in the meeting. She stuck to her guns. The noun is fixed: always guns.
  • Let's bury the axe and move on. Let's bury the hatchet. Don't swap in a synonym.
  • We'll fight fire with water. We'll fight fire with fire. The point is using your opponent's own method.
  • The minister bit the bullet, stuck to her guns and shot down the critics' fort. The minister stuck to her guns. Stacking war idioms sounds like a parody; one is enough.

The big idea

In Metaphors We Live By (1980), linguist George Lakoff and philosopher Mark Johnson used ARGUMENT IS WAR as their first example of a conceptual metaphor: a whole way of thinking that shows up in ordinary words, not just in poetry.

We say a claim is indefensible, a critic attacked a weak point, and someone won the argument. Nobody hears these as figurative; they are simply how English describes disagreement.

The frame has consequences. If debate is war, the other person is an enemy and changing your mind is defeat. Lakoff and Johnson asked readers to imagine a culture where argument is a dance. Skilled speakers can switch frames: explore an idea, build on a point, meet halfway.

You already use it:

  • She attacked every point I made.
  • Frankly, his position is indefensible.
  • The professor demolished my argument in about two minutes.
  • You can never win an argument with my brother.
  • The lawyer found a weak point in the testimony and targeted it.
  • The senator defended her position for over an hour.
  • He retreated from his earlier claim when the data came out.
  • OK, fire away. What's your question?

The expressions

stick to your gunsEveryone said the café would fail, but she stuck to her guns and it's thriving.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: refuse to change your opinion or plan despite pressure neutral

Bend it: Change the tense and possessive: he stuck to his guns, we're sticking to our guns. Keep guns plural.

Note: Usually approving. Compare dig your heels in, which suggests stubbornness rather than conviction.

bite the bulletWe finally bit the bullet and replaced the furnace before winter.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: force yourself to do something unpleasant that can't be avoided neutral

Bend it: Often followed by and + verb: bite the bullet and call him. Tense changes freely; no object after bullet.

Note: Don't confuse it with dodge a bullet (escape harm). Learners mix them up because both contain bullet.

dodge a bulletThe firm I almost joined went bankrupt in March. I really dodged a bullet.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: narrowly escape something bad casual

Bend it: We dodged a bullet, that was a bullet dodged, dodged a big bullet. Usually past tense, looking back.

Note: Often said with relief about a lucky escape, even a relationship: you dodged a bullet with him. A more neutral alternative is a narrow escape.

close ranksWhen the scandal broke, the board closed ranks around the CEO.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: (of a group) unite to protect one another from criticism neutral

Bend it: Close ranks around or behind someone; any tense. Keep ranks plural and with no article.

Note: In news writing it is often mildly critical: insiders protecting one of their own instead of being open.

a double-edged swordWorking from home is a double-edged sword: no commute, but no clear end to the day.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: something that has both a clear benefit and a related drawback neutral

Bend it: A fixed noun phrase. Natural frames: something of a double-edged sword, proved a double-edged sword.

Note: Best when the advantage itself causes the harm. If the pros and cons are unrelated, just say it has pros and cons.

shoot down (an idea)I suggested a four-day week, but my manager shot it down in seconds.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: reject an idea or proposal firmly and quickly casual

Bend it: Separable: shoot down the idea or shoot it down. The passive is very common: my plan got shot down.

Note: A little blunt. In formal writing prefer reject or dismiss. Related: shoot holes in an argument.

bury the hatchetAfter years of silence, the two brothers buried the hatchet at their mother's birthday.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: end a quarrel and become friendly again neutral

Bend it: Any tense or subject: it's time to bury the hatchet. Keep hatchet; never axe.

Note: Usually linked to peace customs of Indigenous nations in northeastern North America, where weapons were buried to mark the end of hostilities.

fight fire with fireWhen the rival campaign ran attack ads, ours decided to fight fire with fire.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: respond to an attack by using the same methods as the attacker neutral

Bend it: Usually in the base form after decide to, have to, you can't. The wording is fixed.

Note: Can sound like a justification for bad behaviour. The opposite choice is to take the high road.

In literature, poetry and music

Writers and songwriters use the same big idea. Read about each one, then find it and read or listen together in the lesson.

Literature

Hamlet

William Shakespeare, c. 1600

Shakespeare gave English hoist with his own petard. Hamlet arranges for the men sent to betray him to be destroyed by their own plot. A petard was a small bomb for blasting open gates, so the image is an engineer blown up by his own device: caught in your own trap.

Can you think of a politician, company or celebrity who was hoist with their own petard?

Literature

Julius Caesar

William Shakespeare, 1599

After Caesar's murder, Mark Antony imagines war being released like hunting dogs straining at the leash. The phrase let slip the dogs of war comes from this speech, and journalists still use it for the start of a conflict, military or political.

Why is an image of hunting dogs so effective for the moment a war begins?

Music

Love Is a Battlefield

Pat Benatar, 1983

The title carries the war frame from debate into romance: a relationship becomes a place of combat, with power struggles, wounds and survival. It shows how easily one conceptual metaphor spreads into new areas of life.

Do people in your first language talk about love as a kind of war? What other metaphors do they use for it?

Music

I Won't Back Down

Tom Petty, 1989

The title uses the language of holding a position under fire. The song is about refusing to give in to pressure, the same idea as stick to your guns or hold the fort, which is why it has been popular at rallies and sports events.

When is refusing to back down admirable, and when is it just stubbornness?

Poetry

Dulce et Decorum Est

Wilfred Owen, 1920

Owen, a British officer killed a week before the 1918 Armistice, describes a gas attack in the First World War. The poem rejects the old idea that dying for your country is sweet and glorious. It is a sober reminder of the real suffering behind our casual war idioms.

Does the reality of war change how you feel about phrases like <em>win an argument</em> or <em>shoot down an idea</em>?
