Common mistakes to watch for

  • We see eye in eye on most things. We see eye to eye on most things. The preposition is to, and the phrase is far more common in the negative.
  • Can you throw light to the problem? Can you shed light on the problem? Shed or throw light on, never to.
  • The managers closed a blind eye to the bullying. The managers turned a blind eye to the bullying. The verb is fixed: turn.
  • I see where are you coming from. I see where you're coming from. It's an embedded question, so no inversion.
  • Nobody told me, so I was in the darkness. I was in the dark. The idiom uses the dark, not the darkness.

The big idea

In English, the mind has eyes. We see what someone means, an explanation is clear or murky, and a clever person is brilliant while a slow one is dim. Lakoff and Johnson listed this pattern as UNDERSTANDING IS SEEING.

The pattern goes back a long way. Plato pictured ignorance as life in a dark cave, and the eighteenth-century movement that celebrated reason is still called the Enlightenment.

Because seeing depends on where you stand, the metaphor also gives us perspective, point of view and angle. Two people can look at the same facts and see different things, which is why this system is so useful for polite disagreement.

You already use it:

  • I see. So the meeting is cancelled, not postponed.
  • Thanks, that was a really clear explanation.
  • The legal situation is still pretty murky.
  • It was an illuminating talk about the history of the city.
  • She's absolutely brilliant at statistics.
  • I have a dim memory of meeting him in Montreal.
  • From my point of view, the deadline is unrealistic.
  • The report offers real insight into rural poverty.

The expressions

shed light onThe new documents shed light on why the bridge collapsed.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: help to explain something that was unclear formal

Bend it: Also throw light on; add adjectives: shed new light on, shed little light on. Past tense is shed.

Note: Common in journalism and academic writing. In conversation people more often say explain or clear up.

in the darkThe staff were kept in the dark about the merger until the last minute.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: not informed about something neutral

Bend it: Usually keep someone in the dark or be left in the dark. Also a shot in the dark (a pure guess).

Note: It suggests information was withheld. If you simply don't know, I have no idea is more natural.

a blind spotHe's a great manager, but he has a blind spot when it comes to his own son.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: an area where someone consistently fails to notice or understand something neutral

Bend it: Have a blind spot about/for/when it comes to. Plural is fine: we all have blind spots.

Note: From the area a driver can't see in the mirrors. It is a gentle, non-insulting way to point out bias.

tunnel visionThe investigators developed tunnel vision and ignored evidence that pointed elsewhere.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: focusing on one thing so much that you ignore everything else neutral

Bend it: Uncountable noun: have/develop tunnel vision. No article: not a tunnel vision.

Note: Usually critical. A positive version of focus is single-minded or laser-focused.

not see eye to eyeMy father and I don't see eye to eye on politics, so we talk about hockey.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: disagree neutral

Bend it: Usually negative: don't/didn't/never see eye to eye on/about/with. Positive use exists but is rare.

Note: A polite, rather mild way to describe ongoing disagreement. It doesn't mean a single argument.

see where you're coming fromI see where you're coming from, but I still think we should wait.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: understand the reasons for someone's opinion casual

Bend it: Change the person: I can see where she's coming from. Often followed by but.

Note: A classic softener before disagreeing. Overused, it can sound like a script; vary it with I take your point.

in my mind's eyeIn my mind's eye I can still see my grandmother's kitchen in Sudbury.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: in my imagination or memory formal

Bend it: Change the possessive: in her mind's eye. Also picture something in your mind's eye.

Note: Slightly literary. Shakespeare's Hamlet made it famous, though earlier writers used similar wording.

turn a blind eyeFor years the league turned a blind eye to violence on the ice.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: deliberately ignore something you know is wrong neutral

Bend it: Turn a blind eye to something; any tense. The verb turn is fixed.

Note: Popularly linked to Admiral Nelson at Copenhagen in 1801, said to have raised his telescope to his blind eye to avoid seeing a signal. The story is likely embellished.

In literature, poetry and music

Writers and songwriters use the same big idea. Read about each one, then find it and read or listen together in the lesson.

Literature

Hamlet

William Shakespeare, c. 1600

Early in the play Hamlet tells his friend Horatio that he can picture his dead father, not with his real eyes but with his mind's eye. Shakespeare made the phrase famous, and it neatly shows how English treats imagination and memory as a kind of inner sight.

What place or person can you see most clearly in your mind's eye? Describe it.

Philosophy

The Republic (the allegory of the cave)

Plato, c. 375 BCE

Plato imagines prisoners chained in a cave who see only shadows on a wall and think they are reality. One prisoner escapes, is dazzled by the sun, and slowly learns to see the real world. Leaving darkness for light becomes a picture of education itself.

Is there something you once believed that now looks like a shadow on the wall to you?

Find it
Music

Both Sides Now

Joni Mitchell, 1969

The Canadian songwriter looks at clouds, then love, then life itself from two opposite points of view. Each time she ends up admitting she doesn't really understand them at all. Seeing from both sides becomes a picture of growing up and of honest uncertainty.

Is there a topic you understood better when you were younger than you do now?

Listen to it
Music

Anthem

Leonard Cohen, 1992

Cohen, from Montreal, suggests that imperfection is not a failure: the broken places in people and the world are exactly where light, and understanding, can enter. It is one of the most quoted ideas in Canadian song.

Do you agree that mistakes and failures help us understand more? Give an example.

Listen to it
Poetry

Amazing Grace

John Newton, 1779

Newton, a former captain of slave ships who later supported abolition, wrote this hymn about his own change of heart. He describes his past as blindness and his new understanding as finally being able to see, the same contrast as the idiom see the light.

Why do you think the idea of blindness and sight is so powerful when people describe a change of belief?
