What this lesson is for

Why this matters: English treats ideas as things we cook, serve, taste and digest. Knowing the system lets you describe how good an idea is, how hard it is to accept and how it is presented.

You will know

  • the conceptual metaphor IDEAS ARE FOOD and its everyday traces
  • eight common food idioms for thinking, accepting and teaching

You will understand

  • why we digest information and call a weak plan half-baked
  • which idioms praise, criticise or simply describe

You will be able to

  • evaluate ideas and proposals with precise, idiomatic language
  • criticise tactfully, for example a plan that is half-baked or news that is hard to swallow
  • avoid common distortions such as the proof is in the pudding in formal writing

Common mistakes to watch for

  • That gave me a food for thought. That gave me food for thought. It's uncountable: no a.
  • The proof is in the pudding. The proof of the pudding is in the eating. The short form is very common in North America, but the full proverb makes the logic clear: you judge by results.
  • His plan is half-cooked. His plan is half-baked. Only baked works in this idiom.
  • I have a lot in my plate this month. I have a lot on my plate. Food sits on a plate.
  • Don't sugar my news, just tell me. Don't sugar-coat it. The verb is sugar-coat.

The big idea

Lakoff and Johnson noticed that English treats ideas like food. We cook up a plan, find a theory hard to swallow, need time to digest a report, and have a taste for history. Data that hasn't been processed is raw.

The metaphor maps the whole meal onto thinking: preparing ideas (half-baked, cooked up), serving them (spoon-feed, sugar-coat), and taking them in (chew over, swallow, digest). Students who only repeat facts regurgitate them, which is not a compliment.

Because eating is so physical, these idioms are vivid and often evaluative. A meaty argument has substance; a half-baked one does not.

You already use it:

  • I need a few days to digest all this information.
  • It's a meaty report with plenty to discuss.
  • We have the raw data, but nobody has analysed it yet.
  • They cooked up a plan to surprise their parents.
  • Don't just regurgitate the textbook. Tell me what you think.
  • She developed a taste for politics at university.
  • I'm still trying to swallow the idea that he's retiring.
  • The documentary left a bitter taste in my mouth.

The expressions

food for thoughtThe speaker's comments on loneliness gave us all food for thought.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: something worth thinking about carefully neutral

Bend it: Uncountable: give someone food for thought, plenty of food for thought, real food for thought.

Note: Polite and positive. In a meeting, That's food for thought can also be a diplomatic way of saying I'm not convinced yet.

half-bakedThe council's half-baked parking scheme lasted three weeks.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: (of an idea or plan) not properly thought out dismissive

Bend it: Adjective before a noun (a half-baked idea) or after be (the plan is half-baked). Fixed: always baked.

Note: Quite critical. To soften, say it needs more work or it isn't fully developed yet.

hard to swallowThe news that the clinic would close was hard to swallow.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: difficult to accept or believe neutral

Bend it: Also difficult to swallow, a bitter pill to swallow, or the verb alone: I can't swallow that excuse.

Note: Can mean emotionally painful (bad news) or not believable (a weak excuse). Context decides.

chew it overIt's a generous offer, but let me chew it over this weekend.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: think about something carefully before deciding casual

Bend it: Separable: chew over the offer / chew it over. Also chew on it.

Note: Friendly and informal. In formal writing use consider or reflect on.

sugar-coatPlease don't sugar-coat it, doctor. How serious is it?What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: make bad news sound better than it really is neutral

Bend it: A verb: sugar-coat the truth, a sugar-coated version. Often negative: I won't sugar-coat this.

Note: Like a sweet coating on a bitter pill. Compare sweeten the deal, which means adding a real benefit.

spoon-feedUniversity lecturers won't spoon-feed you the way some high-school teachers do.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: give people information so simply that they don't have to think dismissive

Bend it: Spoon-feed someone or spoon-feed information to someone; past tense spoon-fed.

Note: Critical of both sides: the teacher who over-helps and the learner who waits to be fed.

have a lot on your plateWith a new baby and a house move, they have a lot on their plate.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: have many tasks or problems to deal with casual

Bend it: Have too much / enough on your plate; with everything on her plate.

Note: A sympathetic way to explain why someone is busy or stressed. British speakers also say have a lot on.

bring something to the tableHer years in Nunavut bring a valuable perspective to the table.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: offer a useful skill, idea or resource neutral

Bend it: Name the thing: bring experience/energy/a fresh perspective to the table. Question: What do you bring to the table?

Note: Very common in job interviews and business. Can sound like jargon if repeated.

In literature, poetry and music

Writers and songwriters use the same big idea. Read about each one, then find it and read or listen together in the lesson.

Literature

Antony and Cleopatra

William Shakespeare, c. 1606

Cleopatra looks back on her earlier romance with Julius Caesar and dismisses it as belonging to her salad days, when she was young, inexperienced and as green as fresh leaves. Shakespeare coined the phrase, which now means a carefree and naive youth.

What were your salad days like? What did you believe then that makes you smile now?

Literature

The Fox and the Grapes

Aesop, c. 6th century BCE

A hungry fox fails to reach a bunch of grapes and walks away telling himself they were probably sour anyway. The fable gave English sour grapes: pretending you never wanted something you couldn't have.

Can you think of a public figure or company whose criticism looked like sour grapes?

Poetry

Harlem

Langston Hughes, 1951

Hughes asks what happens to a dream that is put off for too long, and imagines it spoiling like food: drying up like a raisin in the sun, festering, going rotten. The dream is food that has been left uneaten. The poem gave Lorraine Hansberry the title of her 1959 play A Raisin in the Sun.

What happens to a dream or plan that is postponed for years? Is food a good metaphor for it?

Music

Food for Thought

UB40, 1980

The Birmingham reggae band's first single takes the idiom as its title, but the song is about real hunger: it contrasts Christmas feasting in rich countries with famine elsewhere. The double meaning makes listeners think about literal food and thought at the same time.

Why might a songwriter choose an idiom with a literal meaning that matches the song's subject?
