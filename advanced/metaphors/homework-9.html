Quick reminder

In English, emotional closeness is temperature. Friendly people are warm, distant people are cool or cold, and anger heats up. Linguists link this to early bodily experience: being held close is being kept warm.

  • break the ice: say or do something to ease tension when people first meet
  • get cold feet: become nervous and hesitate about something you planned or promised to do
  • steal someone's thunder: take attention or praise away from someone, often by doing or saying their thing first
  • a storm in a teacup: a lot of anger or worry about something unimportant
  • the calm before the storm: a quiet period just before trouble or intense activity
  • under the weather: slightly ill
  • a frosty reception: an unfriendly, cold response from people
  • take a rain check: politely decline an invitation while suggesting another time

Watch out for

  • I have cold legs about the wedding. I'm getting cold feet about the wedding. Always feet, usually with get or have.
  • Let's cut the ice with a quick game. Let's break the ice with a quick game. The verb is fixed.
  • It was just a storm in a glass of water. It was just a storm in a teacup. The glass of water is a literal translation; Americans say a tempest in a teapot.
