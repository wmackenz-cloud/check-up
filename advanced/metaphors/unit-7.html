Common mistakes to watch for

  • I passed two hours in the airport to kill the time. I killed time at the airport for two hours. Kill time has no article, and the idiom replaces passed two hours.
  • I'm pressing for time today. I'm pressed for time today. It is passive: the time pressure is on you.
  • It's worth of your while to visit. It's worth your while to visit. Worth takes no of.
  • We need to recover the lost time. We need to make up for lost time. This is the fixed, natural form.
  • I lost my time with that course. I wasted my time on that course. Lose time is about delays; waste time is about pointless use.

The big idea

English treats time like money: we have a limited amount, we can spend it well or waste it, save it, invest it, borrow it, or run out of it. A meeting can cost us an afternoon; a shortcut can save us an hour.

This picture grew stronger with clocks, factories and hourly wages, when time literally became something you were paid for. Benjamin Franklin's maxim time is money (1748) sums it up.

Notice the mood it creates. Time is scarce, measured and always slipping away, which is why English has so many idioms about racing the clock and so few about having plenty of time.

You already use it:

  • I spent the whole weekend painting the fence.
  • Don't waste your time arguing with him online.
  • The new highway saves me twenty minutes each way.
  • She invested a lot of time in that friendship.
  • Students need to learn to budget their time.
  • That wrong turn cost us an hour.
  • We ran out of time before the last question.
  • It was a profitable afternoon: we cleared the whole backlog.

Meaning: do something to get more time before a decision or problem arrives neutral

Bend it: Add a person: buy yourself some time. Add amount: buy a few more weeks. Also play for time (British).

Note: Often suggests a delay tactic rather than a solution. Stall is more negative; buy time can be wise or sneaky.

on borrowed timeWith falling ratings, the show has been on borrowed time for a year.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: continuing to exist or live longer than expected, but likely to end soon neutral

Bend it: Usually living / be on borrowed time. Fixed core; don't change borrowed.

Note: Can be serious (illness) or light (an old car). The image is a loan that will soon be called back.

Meaning: having very little time neutral

Bend it: Add degree: a little / very / hard pressed for time. Synonym: short of time.

Note: A polite way to end or shorten a conversation. Less casual than I'm swamped.

Meaning: worth the time or effort it takes neutral

Bend it: Change the pronoun: worth our while. Also make it worth your while (offer a reward, sometimes a bribe).

Note: 'I'll make it worth your while' can hint at an under-the-table payment, so be careful in business.

Meaning: do a lot of something quickly because you couldn't do it earlier neutral

Bend it: Tense changes. Fixed core: keep lost time. Often with trying to.

Note: Can be warm (relationships) or practical (catching up after a strike or a closure).

kill timeWe killed time in the bookshop until the train arrived.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: do something unimportant while waiting casual

Bend it: No article: kill time, not kill the time. Add amount: kill an hour.

Note: Kill time is neutral about waiting. Waste time is critical: it implies you should be doing something useful.

Meaning: trying to finish before a deadline neutral

Bend it: Common verbs: work / race / a race against the clock. Also beat the clock.

Note: Mixes our metaphor with a race: time is both money and an opponent. Very common in news headlines.

time is moneyLet's skip the small talk; time is money.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: time is valuable, so don't waste it casual

Bend it: Fixed: don't change it. Often a reason or excuse: Hurry up, time is money!

Note: Made famous by Benjamin Franklin (1748). Can sound brisk or even rude in social settings; use it humorously among friends.

In literature, poetry and music

Writers and songwriters use the same big idea. Read about each one, then find it and read or listen together in the lesson.

Essay

Advice to a Young Tradesman

Benjamin Franklin, 1748

In this short letter of advice, Franklin explains that a working man who idles away part of a day has not only spent money on his leisure but also lost the wages he could have earned. His maxim time is money turns lost hours into lost income, the core of this metaphor.

Do you agree that free time has a price? How do you decide what an hour of your time is worth?

Music

Time

Pink Floyd, 1973

On The Dark Side of the Moon, the song begins with a crash of clocks and describes drifting through youth assuming there is plenty of time, then suddenly realizing years have gone. Time is a resource wasted without noticing until it runs out.

At what age, if any, did you start to feel that time was running out?

Listen to it
Music

Time in a Bottle

Jim Croce, 1972

A love song from the album You Don't Mess Around with Jim (a number one hit in the US after Croce's death in 1973). The singer wishes he could store time like savings, keep days in a bottle and spend them later with the one he loves.

If you could save time in a bottle, which days would you keep, and who would you spend them with?

Listen to it
Literature

Alice's Adventures in Wonderland

Lewis Carroll, 1865

The White Rabbit is always checking his watch and fretting that he is late. At the tea party, the Hatter treats Time as a person, says he quarrelled with him, and since then it is always six o'clock. Carroll is playing with idioms like killing time and wasting time.

Are you more like the White Rabbit, always late and worried, or the Hatter, stuck at tea time forever?

Find it
Poetry

To His Coy Mistress

Andrew Marvell, 1681

Published after Marvell's death, the poem argues that slow courtship would be fine if there were unlimited time, but time is racing up behind the lovers like a chariot, so they must act now. Time is a resource that is running out.

Is 'life is short, so act now' a good argument, or a pressure tactic? When have you heard it used?
