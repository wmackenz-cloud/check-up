What this lesson is for

Why this matters: Native speakers twist idioms on purpose for humour, headlines and emphasis, and they also garble them by accident. Knowing the difference lets you enjoy wordplay, avoid embarrassing errors and judge when a fixed phrase is better left alone.

You will know

  • the correct forms of eight commonly garbled expressions, such as for all intents and purposes and a moot point
  • the terms mixed metaphor, malapropism, eggcorn and dead metaphor

You will understand

  • that a deliberate twist works only when the original idiom is instantly recognisable
  • why stale cliches weaken writing, and when plain language beats an idiom

You will be able to

  • spot and correct garbled idioms in speech and writing
  • extend or twist a metaphor deliberately for effect
  • decide when not to use an idiom at all

Common mistakes to watch for

  • For all intensive purposes, the deal is done. For all intents and purposes, the deal is done. A classic mishearing, known as an eggcorn.
  • We'll burn that bridge when we come to it. We'll cross that bridge when we come to it. Mixing in burn your bridges is only funny on purpose.
  • Ironically, it rained on the day of our picnic. Unfortunately, it rained on the day of our picnic. Bad luck isn't irony; irony needs a reversal of expectation.
  • The new policy gives managers free reign. The new policy gives managers free rein. From riding: loose reins, not a king's reign.
  • (Cover letter) I'm a team player who thinks outside the box and hits the ground running. Replace stacked cliches with specifics: name a result you achieved. Too many idioms in formal writing sound empty.

The big idea

Idioms are fixed, but not frozen. Natives bend them for effect: headline writers love puns, such as grate expectations for a story about a cheese shop, and comedians extend a familiar image until it becomes absurd. The trick works only because everyone knows the original.

Accidental bending is different. A mixed metaphor joins two images that clash (we'll burn that bridge when we come to it). A malapropism swaps in a similar-sounding wrong word (a mute point). Some speakers turn this into a style: the baseball player Yogi Berra became famous for odd but memorable sayings, now nicknamed Yogi-isms.

Finally, many metaphors are dead: nobody notices that a deadline or the foot of a mountain is figurative. Writers can revive a dead metaphor by taking it literally. George Orwell warned against what he called dying metaphors in between: worn-out images that people use to avoid thinking.

You already use it:

  • We're coming up to the deadline.
  • She's the head of the department.
  • The foot of the mountain was covered in fog.
  • I finally grasp the idea.
  • Traffic hit a bottleneck near the bridge.
  • Prices skyrocketed after the storm.
  • Let's tackle the agenda.

for all intents and purposesFor all intents and purposes, the election was over by nine o'clock.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: in every practical sense; virtually formal

Bend it: Fixed: don't change it, but British English often says to all intents and purposes. Commonly misheard as for all intensive purposes.

Note: Slightly formal and wordy. In concise writing, effectively or in practice is often better.

nip it in the budIf a new hire is rude to customers, nip it in the bud on day one.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: stop something at an early stage neutral

Bend it: The object can change (nip the problem in the bud); nip and bud are fixed.

Note: The garble nip it in the butt is a real error and a popular joke. The image is pinching off a flower bud.

a moot pointWhether we should have hired more staff is a moot point now that the shop has closed.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: a question that no longer matters, or one still open to debate formal

Bend it: Fixed: moot, never mute. Also the point is moot.

Note: In North America it usually means irrelevant; in British usage it can still mean debatable. Context decides.

I couldn't care lessHonestly, I couldn't care less who wins the cooking show.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: I don't care at all dismissive

Bend it: Many Americans say I could care less with the same meaning. It's common but widely criticised; use couldn't in careful speech.

Note: Can sound rude. Softer options: I'm not too bothered or it doesn't really matter to me.

case in pointSmall towns are losing their banks; case in point, ours closed in March.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: an example that proves what you have just said neutral

Bend it: Fixed. Also a case in point. Often misheard and written as case and point.

Note: Used right after a general claim. For example is neutral; case in point sounds more pointed and argumentative.

toe the lineBackbench MPs were told to toe the line on the budget vote.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: obey the rules or official policy, often reluctantly neutral

Bend it: Tense and noun vary: toed the party line. Often misspelled tow the line.

Note: The image is runners with their toes on a starting line. Orwell noted the tow spelling as proof that writers no longer picture the image.

home in onThe investigators quickly homed in on the accounting department.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: move steadily towards a target; focus closely on something neutral

Bend it: Tense varies: homing in on, homed in on. Hone in on is widespread in North America but many editors still mark it as an error.

Note: The image is a homing pigeon or missile. Hone means sharpen, as in hone your skills.

free reinThe new chef was given free rein to redesign the menu.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: complete freedom to act as you choose neutral

Bend it: Fixed noun: give someone free rein, have free rein. Spelled rein, not reign.

Note: From riding: a horse given loose reins can go where it likes. The reign spelling is a very common written error.

In literature, poetry and music

Writers and songwriters use the same big idea. Read about each one, then find it and read or listen together in the lesson.

Essay

Politics and the English Language

George Orwell, 1946

Orwell argued that tired, ready-made phrases let writers avoid thinking. His first rule tells writers to drop any figure of speech they have met many times in print and to find a fresh image or speak plainly instead. He also noticed that people mangle dying metaphors because they no longer picture them.

Is Orwell's rule realistic for everyday business writing? Which cliches would you ban first?

Literature

The Rivals

Richard Brinsley Sheridan, 1775

Mrs Malaprop constantly reaches for impressive words and picks slightly wrong ones that sound similar. She is so memorable that English named the mistake after her: a malapropism. Her name comes from the French mal à propos, meaning inappropriate.

Why do people misuse big words? Have you heard a funny malapropism in English or in your own language?

Literature

Much Ado About Nothing

William Shakespeare, around 1598

Dogberry, a self-important constable, keeps choosing grand words that mean the opposite of what he intends, yet his bumbling night watch accidentally uncovers the villain's plot. Such errors are sometimes called dogberryisms.

Why might a writer let a foolish character solve the mystery? What does that say about cleverness and language?

Poetry

Metaphors

Sylvia Plath, 1960

A nine-line riddle in which every line offers a different metaphor for the speaker, and the hidden answer is pregnancy. The form plays along: nine lines of nine syllables each, echoing nine months. It shows how stacking metaphors can be deliberate and clever, not mixed.

Try to describe yourself, your job or your city as a riddle of three quick metaphors. Can Bill guess it?

Find it
Music

Ironic

Alanis Morissette, 1995

The Ottawa-born singer lists a series of frustrating situations and calls them ironic. Critics and comedians famously pointed out that most are bad luck or bad timing rather than true irony, so the song became a favourite example in debates about what irony really means.

Describe a situation from your life or the news that is truly ironic, not just unlucky. What makes it ironic?
