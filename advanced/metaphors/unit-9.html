What this lesson is for

Why this matters: English speakers constantly describe emotions and relationships as temperature and weather: a warm welcome, a frosty silence, a stormy marriage. Recognising the system helps you read tone instantly and pick the right register.

You will know

  • eight core weather and temperature idioms, such as break the ice and steal someone's thunder
  • the everyday hidden metaphors: warm, cool, heated, icy, sunny, stormy

You will understand

  • that warmth usually means friendliness, while cold means distance, hostility or nervousness
  • that storms stand for conflict and sudden emotion, and calm stands for control

You will be able to

  • choose the right weather idiom for a social or work situation
  • vary these idioms naturally without breaking them
  • discuss songs, poems and plays that turn weather into feeling

Common mistakes to watch for

  • I have cold legs about the wedding. I'm getting cold feet about the wedding. Always feet, usually with get or have.
  • Let's cut the ice with a quick game. Let's break the ice with a quick game. The verb is fixed.
  • It was just a storm in a glass of water. It was just a storm in a teacup. The glass of water is a literal translation; Americans say a tempest in a teapot.
  • I'm under the weather; I broke my leg skiing. Under the weather means mildly ill, not injured: just say I broke my leg skiing.
  • (Formal letter) Regrettably, I must take a rain check on your kind invitation. Regrettably, I am unable to attend. Take a rain check is casual and North American.

The big idea

In English, emotional closeness is temperature. Friendly people are warm, distant people are cool or cold, and anger heats up. Linguists link this to early bodily experience: being held close is being kept warm.

Moods are weather: a sunny disposition, a cloud hanging over someone, a storm of protest. Weather happens to us, so this metaphor often presents feelings as forces we cannot fully control.

Storms also come with a timeline: tension builds, the storm breaks, the air clears. That is why we speak of the calm before the storm or say that an argument cleared the air.

You already use it:

  • The negotiations became heated after midnight.
  • She gave me an icy stare across the boardroom.
  • Relations between the two countries have cooled since the election.
  • He has a sunny personality, even on Monday mornings.
  • A cloud hung over the team after the layoffs.
  • Their friendship warmed over the summer.
  • The minister faced a storm of criticism.
  • Let's talk it through and clear the air.

Meaning: say or do something to ease tension when people first meet neutral

Bend it: Flexible in tense and as a noun: an icebreaker, a good ice-breaking question. Keep break and ice.

Note: Shakespeare used it in The Taming of the Shrew (early 1590s), helping to make it popular. The image is a ship cutting through frozen water to open a route.

get cold feetTwo days before signing the lease, Marco got cold feet and asked for another week.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: become nervous and hesitate about something you planned or promised to do casual

Bend it: Works with get, have or develop, and as a noun: a case of cold feet. Feet stays plural.

Note: Strongly linked to weddings. The near-synonym have second thoughts is milder and more neutral.

steal someone's thunderI was about to share my news, but my sister stole my thunder by announcing her engagement over dessert.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: take attention or praise away from someone, often by doing or saying their thing first neutral

Bend it: The possessive changes freely: steal her thunder, stole the CEO's thunder. Never lightning.

Note: From the critic and playwright John Dennis, who devised a new thunder effect for his play Appius and Virginia (1709). When a rival theatre reused it, he complained they had stolen his thunder.

a storm in a teacupThe row over the office fridge rota turned out to be a storm in a teacup.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: a lot of anger or worry about something unimportant dismissive

Bend it: British and Canadian form; Americans usually say a tempest in a teapot. Don't mix the two halves.

Note: Calling someone else's concern a storm in a teacup can sound dismissive, so be careful using it about another person's worries.

the calm before the stormThe office was strangely silent on Sunday night, the calm before the storm of the year-end audit.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: a quiet period just before trouble or intense activity neutral

Bend it: Often framed as this is just the calm before the storm. Writers sometimes reverse it, the storm before the calm, for effect.

Note: Used half-jokingly about busy seasons at work, but also seriously in news reports about conflict or extreme weather.

under the weatherI'm a bit under the weather, so I'll join the meeting by video.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: slightly ill casual

Bend it: Mostly fixed. Soften or intensify before it: a bit, slightly, feeling under the weather.

Note: A polite, vague way to explain an absence. Using it about a serious illness or a hospital stay sounds oddly understated.

a frosty receptionThe new parking fees got a frosty reception from local shop owners.What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: an unfriendly, cold response from people neutral

Bend it: Both words bend: a chilly reception, a frosty response, the atmosphere was frosty.

Note: Common in journalism and business reporting. The opposite is a warm welcome, not a hot reception.

take a rain checkI'll have to take a rain check on drinks tonight; how about Thursday?What do you think it means? Tap to check.

Meaning: politely decline an invitation while suggesting another time casual

Bend it: Also Can I take a rain check? or simply Rain check? Usually two words; raincheck also appears.

Note: North American, from baseball tickets that let fans return if a game was rained out. British speakers understand it but may say another time.

In literature, poetry and music

Writers and songwriters use the same big idea. Read about each one, then find it and read or listen together in the lesson.

Literature

King Lear

William Shakespeare, around 1606

In Act 3 the old king, rejected by his daughters, wanders across a heath in a violent storm. The wild weather outside mirrors the chaos in his mind and in his kingdom, a classic example of weather standing in for feeling.

Can you think of a film or novel where the weather matches a character's emotions? Does it work, or is it a cliche?

Poetry

Fog

Carl Sandburg, 1916

A tiny poem that compares fog to a cat: it arrives silently, sits looking over the city and harbour, then moves on. Here the metaphor runs the other way, giving weather the quiet, independent mood of an animal.

What animal would you compare a thunderstorm or a heatwave to, and why?

Music

Here Comes the Sun

The Beatles (written by George Harrison), 1969

Harrison wrote it on a spring day after a long English winter and a stressful run of business meetings. The returning sun stands for relief and hope: the mood lifts the way the weather lifts.

Does a particular season or kind of weather change your mood? Describe it with a metaphor.

Music

Ring of Fire

Johnny Cash (written by June Carter and Merle Kilgore), 1963

The song describes falling in love as falling into fire. Passion is heat: exciting and irresistible, but also painful and dangerous. It shows how far the temperature metaphor can stretch, from warm affection to burning desire.

Is love more often warm or hot in the songs you know? What's the difference?

Listen to it
Music

Umbrella

Rihanna, 2007

The singer promises to shelter someone through bad weather. Rain stands for hard times and the umbrella for loyalty and protection, turning a simple object into a symbol of a relationship.

Who has been an umbrella for you in hard times? What everyday object would you choose as a symbol of loyalty?
